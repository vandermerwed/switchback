### Replication
No Many Labs/RRR item targets decomposed judgement directly. The two cited meta-analyses (Grove et al. 2000, k≈136; Ægisdóttir et al. 2006, k=67) are themselves large-scale aggregations across decades of studies and function as the closest thing to a broad "replication" of the clinical-vs-mechanical finding; both point the same direction. Separately — and this matters for the "gut score" framing the roster leans on — Nieuwenstein et al. (2015) is a **pre-registered, adequately powered replication that failed** to find the "unconscious thought advantage" (the "gut beats analysis" claim), which caps any related claim at grade C per tie-break rule 1 if that claim were used. It does not directly test decomposed-judgement, but it undercuts a nearby popular claim the roster's design gestures at (leaving the gut column first/unaided).

### Backfire evidence
Two independent lines of backfire evidence, both about the *side effects* of generating and combining reasons rather than the reliability of mechanical combination itself:
1. Wilson & Schooler (1991): explicitly analyzing reasons for a preference can shift the choice toward reasons that are easy to articulate but not the true determinants of quality, reducing agreement with experts.
2. Uhlmann & Cohen (2005) (DOI verified, quote not independently confirmed): criteria themselves can be constructed post hoc to fit a preferred outcome, which would undermine the "consistency" benefit of decomposition if criteria are chosen after an initial holistic reaction rather than before it — directly relevant to the page's instruction to set criteria before scoring.
The transfer note in claims.md is correct to flag this as **analogical to adjacent**: the literature's criterion is predictive accuracy against a *known* outcome; a personal decision has no such external criterion, so the page's actual claimed benefit (surfacing the gap between the mechanical total and the gut score) is one step removed from what has been tested.

### Grade
**A** · transfer **analogical** · effect **moderate**
Rationale: Two meta-analyses agree. Grove et al. (2000, abstract read) find mechanical prediction about 10% more accurate than clinical prediction, consistently across tasks, judges and data types, and Ægisdóttir et al. (2006, now read via its cached abstract) find a 13% gain in the most stringent sample; Dawes (1979, cached abstract) adds that even improper, equal-weight models beat intuition, which is closest to the page's unweighted total. No failed large replication of the mechanical advantage was found (Nieuwenstein et al. 2015 failed to replicate the unconscious-thought advantage, a neighbouring claim, so it does not cap this one). That meets A. Tie-break: none; Ægisdóttir et al. report moderators but no I², so tie-break 4 cannot be tested. Transfer is analogical: the tested criterion is predictive accuracy against a known outcome, whereas a personal decision has none, and the backfires bear on use (analysing reasons can shift choice to easy-to-state criteria, Wilson & Schooler 1991; criteria get built to fit a favourite unless fixed first, Uhlmann & Cohen 2005, cached abstract). Only abstracts were read.

### Adversarial pass
downgraded A→B: tie-break 4, since both meta-analyses report heterogeneity well above I² = 75% that no moderator matching our use explains (Grove et al. 2000, full text: QT = 1635.2 on 135 df, I² ≈ 92%; Ægisdóttir et al. 2006, Table 2: Q = 470.71 on 91 df, I² ≈ 81%, and "the study design characteristics that we hypothesized would influence the overall effect size ... did not reduce the heterogeneity"), and tie-break 7 makes `effect: small` mandatory (d ≈ .12–.16, "a small effect by conventional standards"), with sources Grove et al. 2000 (10.1037/1040-3590.12.1.19) and Ægisdóttir et al. 2006 (10.1177/0011000005285875).

## anchoring
**Claim:** A number written or seen earlier pulls later numeric judgements towards it, so a gut score written *before* criteria scoring cannot be anchored by the total.
**Construct:** the anchoring effect (including self-generated anchors) · **Used by:** options-criteria (LB) · forecast (S)
**Load-bearing:** yes (options-criteria)

### Replication
This is one of the most convincingly replicated effects available: Many Labs 1 (Klein et al. 2014) replicated all four Jacowitz & Kahneman anchoring items in 36 independent samples (5 of the top effect sizes in the whole 13-effect project were anchoring variants), with the replicated effect sizes **larger**, not smaller, than the originals. No failed large replication of anchoring itself was found.

### Backfire evidence
The relevant boundary is not "does anchoring exist" (settled, above) but "does the mechanism still apply to a self-generated anchor written by the same person, in the same order the page uses." Epley & Gilovich (2001) show the classic "insufficient adjustment" account applies specifically to **self-generated** anchors (the mechanism options-criteria depends on, since the page's own earlier gut score is the anchor, not an experimenter-given one) — this strengthens transfer rather than weakening it, but it also means the phenomenon options-criteria is trying to neutralise (the gut score anchoring the criteria total) is exactly the self-generated-anchor case with the best-supported mechanism.
No direct test was found of the page's actual intervention — writing the gut score *first*, before criteria scores, specifically to prevent the *total* from anchoring the gut impression (i.e., using order to try to neutralise anchoring in the *other* direction). This is a genuine gap: the literature establishes that earlier numbers anchor later ones, which supports the page's ordering logic in principle, but no study was located that tests whether reordering in this way actually prevents contamination, or whether consistency pressure (wanting the total and the gut score to agree) reintroduces the very convergence the page exists to reveal. Flagging as "none found (searched: Jacowitz & Kahneman follow-ups, anchoring-order literature via PubMed/CrossRef/Semantic Scholar within this session's tool budget)."

### Grade
**A** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **large**
Rationale: Many Labs 1 (Klein et al. 2014) replicated all four anchoring items across 36 samples, with weighted effects larger than the original (d = 1.17–2.42 against 0.93) and no sample in the wrong direction, which meets A through its "several large pre-registered replications" limb. Epley & Gilovich (2001, abstract read) show that self-generated anchors, the page's case, work through insufficient adjustment. Tie-break: none. The claim's second clause, that a gut score written first cannot be anchored by a total computed later, follows from the order itself. The live risk runs the other way: the gut score, written first, may anchor the criteria scores so the total converges on it, and no read source tests whether the gut-first order hides the mismatch the page exists to show; that belongs in `backfires`. Transfer adjacent, since the replicated anchors were experimenter-provided numbers on almanac questions.

### Adversarial pass
downgraded A→B: the claim as written covers any number "written or seen earlier", but anchors that are only seen, without being considered, failed three high-powered replications ("No statistically significant evidence of incidental anchoring was obtained. In contrast, robust standard anchoring effects were found ... anchoring is limited to situations which require explicit thinking about the anchor"); Many Labs 1 supports A only for considered anchors, so rewording to "a number one has written or compared against" would restore A (the lead's call), with source Shanks, Barbieri-Hermitte & Vadillo 2020 (10.1525/collabra.310).

Veinott, Klein & Wiggins (2010), "Evaluating the Effectiveness of the PreMortem Technique on Plan Confidence" (ISCRAM 2010 conference paper) is the seminal experimental test (178 participants, H1N1 university-response plan, conditions: standard critique / pros-and-cons / cons-only / premortem). It is recorded here as **unverified**, with the finding read via a secondary summary (scienceblog.com, fetched directly): "The premortem group's confidence in the plan dropped by roughly 25 points more than the baseline condition, and reliably more than the standard critique condition." The Gallop et al. (2016) and Peabody dissertation studies above are treated as the verified stand-ins for a "large-n" test of the same technique, and are broadly consistent with Veinott et al.'s unverified finding.

### Backfire evidence

### Grade
**C** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: Only one of the claim's two halves has read evidence. Few studies, mostly of teams, one null comparison, and no admissible evidence for the overconfidence half gives C. Tie-break: none. ### Adversarial pass

(2014) is itself a large field study (not a lab replication) that supports the mechanism as one trained component among several, which weakens attribution of its effect to reference-class use alone. The Flyvbjerg 2013 review (now fetched) reports the Lovallo & Kahneman 2003 outside-view manipulation as a **direct, replicated (across two independent research programmes: Buehler/Griffin/Ross and Gilovich/Griffin/Kahneman) demonstration that outside-view prompts improve accuracy**, which is the closest thing to a "replication" of the core reference-class mechanism found this session.

### Backfire evidence
**Gap-fill resolution (2026-09-25):** the evidence on "does the base rate over-anchor, or does it genuinely correct" splits into two distinct findings, both now sourced:
1. **The outside view can genuinely correct, not just anchor**, when the person is made to actually use the reference information as a benchmark (Flyvbjerg 2013, quoting Lovallo & Kahneman 2003: a simple diversion into outside-view information reduced overconfident forecasts by 20%, moving them *toward* accuracy, not just toward an arbitrary number). This is real evidence for the forecast page's design, not merely a plausible mechanism.
2. **The opposite failure mode is at least as well documented: people ignore the reference point.** Buehler, Griffin & MacDonald (1997, WebSearch synthesis, not yet independently fetched verbatim) found that merely prompting recall of past experience did *not* reduce the planning fallacy — only 12% of participants spontaneously connected it to their current estimate, and the bias persisted until participants were forced to actively link the past experience to the plan at hand. This suggests the risk for `forecast` is less "the reference-class number anchors and distorts the estimate" (the risk claims.md's brief and the `anchoring` claim above worry about) and more "the reference-class line gets written and then quietly discounted as not applicable to this case" — a different, and arguably more likely, failure mode than anchoring.
3. Flyvbjerg 2013 also supplies a genuine **boundary condition** not previously sourced: the outside view helps when forecasters are honestly trying to be accurate (optimism-bias case), but does little when inaccuracy is motivated/strategic (their example: competitive grant or project bids) — a distinction the grading step should weigh if forecast's predictions could ever be motivated (e.g., wanting to look good in a review) rather than purely epistemic.
4. This softens, rather than resolves, the design tension the earlier sourcing pass flagged between `anchoring` and `reference-class`: the risk of the base rate becoming a sticky, hard-to-adjust anchor (the `anchoring` claim's well-replicated mechanism) and the risk of it being written and then ignored are **both plausible**, and no source located this session tests forecast's exact manipulation (a written reference-class line placed first, in one's own hand, before a specific-case forecast) to say which risk dominates in that setup.

### Grade
**C** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994, now read via its cached abstract) found the optimistic bias "eliminated for Ss instructed to connect relevant past experiences with their predictions", a reference class of one's own past. Mellers et al. (2014) taught reference classes as one ingredient of a training package that worked, and Flyvbjerg (2013, full text read) reports Lovallo & Kahneman's (2003) outside-view experiment second-hand. Buehler, Griffin & MacDonald (1997) does not count: its cached abstract is about incentives worsening optimism, not the recall-condition finding recorded from a search synthesis, which looks misattributed. Few studies, consistent in direction, gives C. Tie-break: none. Supporting claim, so it does not move forecast's grade. The cache dates Flyvbjerg 2012 (the online-first year) against the recorded 2013; check-dois should settle it.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed C: I confirm the grader's flag that Buehler, Griffin & MacDonald 1997's cached abstract concerns incentives and does not report the recall-condition finding, which matches Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994 Study 4; with it excluded, one experiment plus a training package and a second-hand report gives C.

### Replication
Roy, Mitten & Christenfeld (2008) is itself a direct, multi-experiment demonstration that the *specific mechanism* forecast `duration` relies on — feedback on actual vs. predicted duration improving subsequent estimates — replicates across three different task types (paper-counting, essay-writing, and a field sample at fast-food restaurants/arcades). No large pre-registered RRR of the planning fallacy itself was located in this pass.

### Backfire evidence
None of the sources directly test the two backfire questions the brief raised (defensive padding once estimates are tracked; feedback correcting the bias only for familiar tasks). Roy et al. (2008)'s Experiment 3 used unfamiliar tasks and strangers' data (not even the person's own history) and still found feedback effective, which is suggestive evidence *against* the "familiar tasks only" concern, but this is an inference, not a direct test — flagged for the grading step.

### Grade
**B** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: Three read sources line up. Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994, now read via its cached abstract) establish the underestimation and show it was eliminated when people connected past experiences to the prediction; Roy, Christenfeld & McKenzie's (2005) review argues people underestimate future durations because they underestimate past ones; and Roy, Mitten & Christenfeld (2008) found that supplying actual durations before a new prediction improved accuracy in all three experiments, including one with strangers' averages. Several consistent experiments from two research groups, without a meta-analysis or large replication, gives B. Tie-break: none. Transfer is adjacent rather than direct: in Roy et al. the experimenter supplied the actual duration, whereas the page has the person record it, and repeated same-day loops are untested; whether tracked estimates get padded is open. Only abstracts were read.

### Adversarial pass
downgraded B→C: the feedback mechanism is mixed, because across three experiments "duration feedback did not influence bias" and only task similarity did (Thomas & König 2018), against the positive results of Roy, Mitten & Christenfeld 2008 and König et al. 2014/2015 (which found that retrospectively estimating the first task's duration reduced underestimation of the next), with sources Thomas & König 2018 (10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00760) and König, Wirz, Thomas & Weidmann (10.1007/s12144-014-9236-3).
