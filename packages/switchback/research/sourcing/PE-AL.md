### Replication
The Murdoch et al. (2023) pre-registered meta-analysis is itself the strongest replication-style evidence: small effect (g = 0.19), explicitly flagged as uncertain given risk of bias in the underlying literature. No separate RRR/Many-Labs test of a single self-distancing study was found.

### Backfire evidence
Murdoch et al. (2023) note quality/risk-of-bias concerns across the literature ("uncertainty regarding the benefit... need for future high-powered, high-quality experiments") rather than a specific reversal. No direct evidence was found in this session that distancing becomes avoidance or blunts emotion that carries useful information (a question the brief raised); this is a gap, not a confirmed backfire.

### Replication
**Gap-fill update (2026-09-25) — resolved.** Sparrow, Liu & Wegner (2011) was indeed among the studies targeted for replication under the Social Sciences Replication Project (Camerer et al. 2018, *Nature Human Behaviour*, DOI 10.1038/s41562-018-0399-z, re-verified via Crossref this session: title/authors/year match). The SSRP replication attempt targeted the paper's "Google Stroop effect" (the priming/interference sub-finding, not the "lower recall of information itself" sub-finding), led by Holzmeister & Camerer, and **it failed to replicate**. Confirmed via a directly-fetched source — Hesselmann (2020), *PeerJ*, DOI 10.7717/peerj.10325 (verified via Crossref this session: title "No conclusive evidence that difficult general knowledge questions cause a 'Google Stroop effect'. A replication study", author Hesselmann, year 2020) — whose abstract, read directly via PMC (PMC7651475), states: "A much-cited study published in *Science* in 2011 provided first behavioral evidence for such changes in human cognition... the 'Google Stroop effect' could not be replicated in two recent replication attempts as part of a large replicability project. After the failed replication was published in 2018, the first author of the original study pointed out some problems with the design of the failed replication." The same fetched source further states, describing the SSRP attempt specifically: "this replication... did not show a significant effect despite adequate statistical power." Hesselmann (2020) is itself a **second, independent** replication attempt (incorporating the original author's methodological objections to the SSRP version) and also found "no conclusive evidence in favor of the notion that the concept of the Internet or internet access... is automatically activated when participants are faced with hard trivia questions." **This is a confirmed failed large replication of the priming/Stroop sub-finding specifically** (Camerer et al.'s SSRP replications used samples ~5x the original on average). Per §6.2 tie-break 1, this caps that sub-finding at C. Note the scope: this bears on the "primed to think about computers" mechanism, not directly on the separate "lower recall of the information itself, better recall of where to find it" transactive-memory finding from the same paper, which was not the subject of either replication attempt found this session — that sub-finding (closer to what player-aid/dashboard/question-queue actually lean on) remains unreplicated either way, a genuine gap.

### Backfire evidence
Built into the claim itself (weaker memory for offloaded content — Sparrow et al. 2011; Storm & Stone 2015's effect is conditional on the saving process being reliable). Meyerhoff et al. (2021) adds a caution that offloading tendencies don't generalize well across tasks/individuals. The brief's question about "keeping the store up to date becoming a second job" was not resolved with a source in this session — a gap, not a confirmed finding. **Gap-fill note (2026-09-25):** the now-confirmed failed replication of the Stroop/priming sub-finding is itself a backfire-relevant caution for how much weight cognitive-offload's Sparrow et al. citation should carry — the grading pass should distinguish the (replicated-elsewhere, more behavioural) transactive-memory/recall-location finding from the (failed-replication) automatic-priming finding when writing the rationale.

### Replication
**This claim is contested.** Ruiz Pardo & Minda (2022) is a pre-registered *direct* replication of Ward et al.'s (2017) own second experiment and found no effect — this triggers §6.2 tie-break 1 (a failed large/pre-registered replication caps the grade at C). Hartmann et al. (2020) is an independent conceptual failure-to-replicate on related memory tasks. Against that, Böttger et al.'s (2023) meta-analysis of 22 studies finds a significant overall negative effect, but with heterogeneity across cognitive domains (possible tie-break 4 territory — I² not reported in the abstract, so I cannot confirm whether it exceeds 75%).

### Backfire evidence
Hartanto & Yang (2016) is listed by the brief as evidence that separation itself carries a cost for high-dependency users (title/metadata only, not confirmed by quote in this session). Stothart et al. (2015) offers an "adjacent alternative" mechanism (notifications, not mere presence) that would let the cover's phone-line be re-grounded even if the mere-presence effect itself does not hold up.

## attention-load/stop-mid-task

### Replication
Ghibellini & Meier (2025) is the meta-analysis the brief anticipated ("lead: Ghibellini & Meier, a recent meta-analysis") — I can now confirm the year (2025) and venue (*Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* 12), which the brief did not specify. **This is good news for the claim as Longhand actually uses it**: the claim as stated in claims.md is about *resumption*, not memory recall, and the meta-analysis directly supports a resumption tendency ("Ovsiankina effect represents a general tendency") while explicitly finding *no* memory (Zeigarnik) advantage. The spec's expectation of "C/D" may be pessimistic for the resumption half of the construct specifically, though the memory-recall half is confirmed null.

### Backfire evidence
Masicampo & Baumeister (2011) shows unfinished/open tasks do cause intrusive thoughts — consistent with the brief's flagged tension with brain-dump's offload claim — but also shows this is eliminated by *making a specific plan*, which is functionally what a resumption cue (next claim) provides. This suggests stop-mid-task's benefit may depend on pairing it with a resumption cue rather than standing alone.

## attention-load/resumption-cues
**Claim:** Leaving a visible cue of where you were and what comes next at the point of interruption reduces the time and errors involved in resuming a task.
**Construct:** memory for goals; resumption lag · **Used by:** dashboard (S) · Sitting (S) · cover (S)
**Load-bearing:** no

| Monk, Trafton & Boehm-Davis (2008) | 10.1037/a0014402 | 2008 | experiment | 3 experiments, interleaved-task paradigm | resumption time scales with interruption duration/demand | "The time to resume task goals after an interruption varied depending on the duration and cognitive demand of interruptions, as predicted by the memory for goals model... longer and more demanding interruptions led to longer resumption times... supported the role of decay in defining resumption costs, and the interaction between duration and demand supported the importance of goal rehearsal in mitigating de[cay]." | **Hodgetts & Jones (2006)** | 10.1037/0278-7393.32.5.1120 | 2006 | experiment | Tower of London interruption paradigm | facilitation vs. impairment depending on cue integrity | "Retrieval of the suspended goal was facilitated when participants were given the opportunity to encode retrieval cues during an 'interruption lag'... but was impeded when these visual cues were subsequently altered following interruption. The results provide useful support for the goal-activation model..." (read: abstract; JEP:LMC 32(5):1120–1132) |

### Replication
Not a single-effect replication design, but four independent studies (Altmann & Trafton 2002; Trafton et al. 2003; Monk et al. 2008; Hodgetts & Jones 2006) from overlapping but distinct labs converge on the same goal-activation model, which is a reasonable proxy for convergent validity even without a formal RRR.

### Backfire evidence
Monk et al. (2008) directly answers the brief's "do cues decay in value over long gaps?" question in the affirmative for interruptions up to ~1 minute (resumption cost scales with duration and demand) — but Longhand's gaps are days to weeks, several orders of magnitude longer than anything tested here. This is the clearest **transfer gap** in this batch: the mechanism is well-supported at lab timescales (seconds to a minute), and Longhand is relying on it holding at timescales the literature has not tested. Hodgetts & Jones (2006) shows that altering the cue after it's set actively *impedes* resumption relative to no cue at all — a caution that a stale or ambiguous cue could be worse than none.
