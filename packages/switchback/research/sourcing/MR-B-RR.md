### Replication
No dedicated Many Labs / RRR / large pre-registered replication of the core self-explanation effect was found in this session (searched). ### Backfire evidence
Schworm & Renkl 2006 is itself the backfire case the brief asked about: adding instructional (given) explanations alongside self-explanation prompts crowds out the learner's own self-explanation activity and *reduces* the outcome self-explanation alone would produce — a "less scaffolding is more" finding that bears directly on how much the AI should annotate self-explain material versus leaving it to the person. Rittle-Johnson et al. 2017 additionally flags that self-explanation's classroom and delayed-retention evidence is weaker than its immediate-post-test evidence, which matters for whether self-explain's benefit survives to the next return.

No dedicated large pre-registered replication of fading specifically was found in this session (searched); the expertise-reversal effect itself (Kalyuga et al. 2003) is a synthesis of many original interaction studies rather than a single replicated experiment, and is treated here as a review/theory-adjacent source.

### Backfire evidence
The expertise-reversal effect (Kalyuga et al. 2003) is itself the backfire condition self-explain's `faded` variant must respect: fading and worked examples that help novices can actively hurt learners who already have relevant schemas, because processing a redundant fully-worked step imposes extraneous load once the learner would otherwise generate the step efficiently from memory. This directly bears on self-explain's claim that `faded` should not be applied indiscriminately to material the person already knows well.

## explanatory-depth

### Replication
The **core** IOED effect (Rozenblit & Keil 2002: attempting a mechanistic explanation lowers self-rated understanding) has independent support from a different research programme and different materials/theory (Alter, Oppenheimer & Zemla 2010's construal-level studies presuppose and reproduce the basic self-rating drop before manipulating construal level), and is very widely cited without a known large-scale failed direct replication in this session's search. The **downstream** claim that this self-rating drop also *moderates political attitudes* (Fernbach et al. 2013) failed to replicate in three preregistered studies with adequate power (Crawford & Ruscio 2021). Per the brief's instruction, these two claims are kept separate: feynman's mechanism relies only on the core self-rating-drop effect, not on any attitude-moderation claim, so Crawford & Ruscio's failure does not by itself apply tie-break rule 1 to feynman's grade — but it is a caution against assuming exposing an explanatory gap changes downstream behaviour or belief, only that it changes the self-rating.

### Backfire evidence
Fernbach et al. 2013's own design shows a boundary condition directly relevant to feynman: the effect requires a **mechanistic** explanation attempt, not merely listing reasons or opinions — "enumerate reasons" did not reduce the illusion. This matters for how feynman's prompt should be worded (ask for a mechanism/process, not a list of justifications). No source located in this session directly tested whether a *fluent but wrong* explanation raises false confidence rather than lowering it (the brief's other backfire question); this remains an open gap and should be searched further at the grading/adversarial stage if it matters to the final grade.

### Replication
No dedicated Many Labs / RRR replication of learning-by-teaching or learning-by-explaining was found in this session (searched). Kobayashi 2019's meta-analysis is the strongest available synthesis and itself aggregates many independent studies, which partially substitutes for a single large replication.

### Backfire evidence
**Gap-fill correction (2026-09-25):** the previous version of this section read Hoogerheide et al. 2016 and Lachner et al. 2018 as showing that written explaining (feynman's own medium) is simply weaker than oral/video explaining. Having now fetched primary or author-review text for both:
- Hoogerheide et al. 2016's own finding is narrower than its title suggests: written explaining did not significantly beat restudy, but the written-vs-oral *difference itself* was not statistically significant either — the study is underpowered to conclude written explaining is worse, not confirmation that it is.
- Lachner, Ly & Nückles 2018 (now directly quoted, above) found a genuine trade-off, not a simple written deficit: written explanations produced **better** organization and conceptual knowledge; oral explanations produced **better** transfer via elaboration. For feynman, a written diagnostic page, this is actually reassuring for the conceptual-knowledge half of the claim, and only a caution for transfer specifically — a materially different conclusion from "written explaining underperforms."
- This should be flagged for the grading step: the backfire risk for feynman's written medium is real but narrower and more specific (transfer, not general understanding) than previously stated.

### Replication
Sinha & Kapur 2021's meta-analysis is itself the field's replication-level synthesis: it aggregates 53 studies and finds the PS-I/productive-failure advantage is **not** nulled by publication-bias correction (the bias-corrected estimate is larger, not smaller, than the raw estimate — an unusual direction worth flagging for the grading step, since tie-break rule 2 (§6.2) concerns bias-corrected estimates that go to zero or reverse, which is not what happened here). No dedicated Many Labs/RRR of the generation effect itself was found in this session (searched), though it is one of the most robustly replicated findings in the memory literature by informal consensus of citing sources.

### Backfire evidence
Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006 is the field's central counter-argument and directly answers the brief's backfire question: minimally guided, discovery-style, or generate-before-instruction designs can underperform guided instruction, particularly for **novices** and for material with **high element interactivity** (many interacting parts that cannot be inferred without prior schemas). Sinha & Kapur 2021's own moderator analysis is consistent with a bounded version of this concern: PS-I's advantage did *not* hold for younger learners (2nd–5th graders) or for domain-general skills, where instruction-first (I-PS) was favored instead. For question-queue, this bounds the claim: deferring the AI's answer until after the person has attempted a question works best when the person has enough relevant background to attempt productively; on genuinely novel, high-complexity material with no foothold, deferring may cost more than it teaches — a live risk the brief itself names ("A deferred question that blocks the work can stall the sitting").

### Replication
The consistent-vs-varied-mapping effect (Schneider & Shiffrin 1977 / Shiffrin & Schneider 1977) is one of the most heavily replicated paradigms in cognitive psychology's automaticity literature (visual search, categorization, skill acquisition), by broad consensus of the field, though no single named large-scale pre-registered replication was located in this session (searched). ### Backfire evidence
Sarter & Woods 1995 supplies the specific backfire mechanism the brief asked about: a fixed mapping applied in a context it fits poorly is not merely suboptimal but can actively mislead, because operators develop automatic, unreflective responses to a mark/control based on its usual meaning, and a context-dependent shift in what it means (a "mode") is exactly what evades detection by trained, attentive users — it is not a novice error. Applied to proof-actions: if the colour-language protocol were to let a colour's meaning shift between artifact types (e.g., "go deeper" on green in one context vs a different meaning on another page type), this predicts systematic misreadings precisely because the mapping usually holds, not despite it.
