### Replication
No large pre-registered RRR of transfer-appropriate processing itself was found. The clearest "failed-to-replicate the naive expectation" result is Sala & Gobet's own finding that far-transfer effects collapse toward zero once active control groups are used — this functions as boundary evidence against overclaiming transfer, not a replication of Morris et al.'s specific effect.

### Backfire evidence
Brady's contextual-interference meta-analysis is a genuine *tension* with the claim as stated: **varying** practice conditions (high contextual interference) that do **not** resemble the eventual test context reliably improve retention and transfer (g ≈ .38, larger in basic research), relative to blocked practice that matches conditions trial-to-trial. So "make practice resemble the target" needs the caveat the brief anticipated: matching *cues and demanded actions* to the target (transfer-appropriate processing) is not the same axis as matching *trial-to-trial conditions* (contextual interference/variability of practice), and the second sometimes cuts the other way. Smith & Vela's meta-analysis also bounds the claim: context effects shrink or vanish once encoding/retrieval encourage non-contextual (introspective) processing — i.e., practice-audit's benefit should be framed as "resemble the *processing demands*," not "resemble every incidental circumstance."

### Backfire evidence
Watkins (2008) is the direct evidentiary hook for the brief's rumination worry: concrete, specific processing (of which "one concrete recent instance" is an example) is generally the *constructive* pole and abstract, evaluative processing (asking "why," dwelling on causes/meanings) is the *unconstructive* pole. This actually cuts **for** frame-by-frame's concreteness requirement rather than against it — but only if the reconstruction stays concrete (what happened, when, where) rather than sliding into abstract "why does this always happen to me" rumination, which Watkins (2009) and Williams et al. (2007) both flag as the failure mode overgeneral/abstract recall represents. The tie between "concreteness training reduces rumination and dysphoria" (Watkins et al. 2009) and "chain analysis demands concreteness" is analogical, not a direct test of chain analysis.

No RRR of choice bracketing was found. Hofmeyr et al. ### Replication
Rogers & Monsell (1995) and Monsell's (2003) review represent one of the most heavily replicated basic-cognition effects in the literature (switch costs are robust and textbook-standard); the applied extension to real-world "media multitasking," however, **fails to replicate reliably**: Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein's own 2 replication attempts plus meta-analysis of 39 effect sizes found the association "turned nonsignificant after correction for small-study effects."

### Backfire evidence
This is the key nuance for Sitting's design rationale: the basic lab switch-cost effect (millisecond-scale, simple two-task alternation) is robust, but the applied, real-world extension the brief's "media multitaskers" lead (Ophir, Nass & Wagner 2009, not independently re-sourced this pass — cited only via Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein's own review of it) does **not** hold up under replication. Sitting's "one move per page" design is closer to the robust lab paradigm (discrete, coarse-grained moves) than to the applied media-multitasking claim, but the transfer is still, per the brief, "analogical" — lab switch costs are measured in milliseconds on trivial classification tasks, and Sitting's moves take minutes. Leroy's (2009) attention-residue finding is the one source that bridges toward the coarser, minutes-scale claim Sitting actually needs, but was only read via secondary paraphrase this pass. The brief's own tension (does breaking off one task help incubation?) was not independently resolved here; it is cross-referenced to creativity-fixation/incubation-effect, sourced in a different batch (CF).
