### Replication
No Many Labs / RRR of random-stimuli-in-ideation specifically was found. The closest large-scale synthesis is the Sio, Kotovsky & Cagan (2015) meta-analysis of 43 studies (above), which functions as an aggregation rather than a pre-registered replication, and Vasconcelos & Crilly's (2016) 25-study integrative review, which explicitly reports "great variety in the methods used and the results obtained" — i.e., the underlying literature is heterogeneous rather than convergent (relevant to §6.2 tie-break 4).

### Backfire evidence
- Jansson & Smith (1991, above): the seminal demonstration that exposure to an example *causes* fixation on that example's features, even when its flaws are pointed out — direct evidence that stimuli can narrow rather than widen output.
- Vasconcelos & Crilly (2016, above) and the broader design-fixation literature it reviews document that whether a stimulus inspires or fixates depends heavily on manipulated variables (source concreteness/abstractness, similarity to the target problem, number of examples shown) rather than being a uniform effect.
- Leahy et al. (2020, above): a targeted mitigation (Design Heuristics) reduced fixation on both provided and self-generated initial ideas — suggesting random/generic prompts may work better as heuristic operators (see `heuristic-prompts`) than as raw stimuli, if the mechanism is disrupting fixation on a single first idea rather than adding novel content per se.

### Grade
**C** · transfer **analogical** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: No read source tests random, unrelated words or prompts, which is what the claim is about. Dugosh et al. (2000, abstract read) show that exposure to *other people's ideas* enhances idea generation, and that "presence of irrelevant information" moderated the effect. That is adjacent at best, and it may cut against unrelated stimuli. Vasconcelos & Crilly (2016, abstract read) find "great variety in the methods used and the results obtained" across 25 studies. Jansson & Smith (1991, via Leahy et al.'s fetched abstract) show that a provided example can fixate designers on its features. Sio, Kotovsky & Cagan (2015) is still unread and is not used. The result is few studies with mixed results, on a neighbouring manipulation, which is C on the base definitions. Tie-break: none (no meta-analysis was read, so tie-breaks 2–4 cannot be tested). Transfer is set to analogical rather than claims.md's adjacent, because the tested stimuli are relevant ideas and design examples, not random words.
Suggested rewording: "Exposure to other ideas or examples during idea generation can raise idea output, but it can also fix attention on the example; which happens depends on the stimulus." This would earn **C** (adjacent).

### Adversarial pass
downgraded C→D: no counted source tests randomly chosen, unrelated words or prompts, which is what the claim asserts. Dugosh et al. exposed people to other people's ideas, and found that the "presence of irrelevant information" moderated the effect; Leahy et al. show that provided examples fixate designers. The manipulation itself is untested craft, the same ruling the grader applied to bad-ideas-criterion (10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.722; 10.1115/1.4046446). The grader's rewording (other ideas or examples) would still earn C.

### Replication
No dedicated large-scale or pre-registered replication of "prompting with generic heuristics increases creative output" was found this session (searched via Crossref bibliographic search for replication/registered-replication terms combined with "design heuristics"). The Yilmaz et al. (2016) heuristics catalog has been used and partially validated in several follow-up studies (Yilmaz, Seifert, Daly & Gonzalez 2016, DOI 10.1115/1.4032219, verified via Crossref but not independently read this session), but these are extensions rather than replications of a causal prompting effect.

### Backfire evidence
The brief asks whether a randomly assigned heuristic that misfits the problem wastes the roll, and whether analogical transfer is poor without an explicit prompt to apply it. No study directly tests "wasted die rolls." On the second question, the well-established Gick & Holyoak (1980, 1983) result — that spontaneous analogical transfer between a source and target is low unless solvers are explicitly told or hinted to use the source — supports the *design rationale* for the die (it converts an implicit, low-transfer analogy task into an explicit prompt), but this is inferred from secondary knowledge of the literature rather than a verbatim-quoted finding, since the two 1980s abstracts could not be fetched this session.

### Grade
**C** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: Two read sources bear on the claim. Yilmaz et al. (2016, abstract read) is an extraction study of what designers already do, and says the 77 heuristics "can help". It is not a test of prompting against no prompting. Leahy et al. (2020, abstract read) is the only experiment: after a first phase, a second 30-minute phase using Design Heuristics showed "less fixation". But the design has no no-heuristic second phase, so the effect is confounded with order. It is also exactly what the serial-order effect would predict anyway. Gick & Holyoak (1980, 1983) are unread and not used. One weakly controlled experiment plus an observational catalogue is "few or small studies", which is C. Tie-break: none. The tested heuristics are a curated card deck of 77, not six generic operators rolled at random, so transfer is adjacent at best.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed: one order-confounded experiment (Leahy et al. 2020) plus an observational catalogue (Yilmaz et al. 2016) meets C's "few or small studies" and no more; both findings match the cached abstracts.

Taken together, three independent groups each narrow the sleep-insight effect to a specific sleep stage or difficulty condition, which is a coherent (rather than contradictory) picture, but each boundary condition also means the effect is considerably narrower than the general claim as stated ("sleeping... increases the likelihood") — relevant to the spec's own expectation of a C grade here.

### Grade
**C** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **large**
Rationale: Four independent experiments were read (abstracts): Wagner et al. (2004), Cai et al. (2009), Sio, Monaghan & Ormerod (2013) and Lacaux et al. (2021). Each finds that some sleep beats wake for insight or associative problems, and the reported effects are large ("more than twice as many"; 83% vs 30%). The results are mixed on the claim as written, though. Non-REM naps did not beat quiet rest (Cai), deeper sleep abolished the N1 benefit (Lacaux), and easy problems showed no sleep benefit (Sio et al.). The samples are small, no meta-analysis was found, and no replication of Wagner et al. (2004) was found. On the base definitions ("few or small studies, mixed results") this is C, as the spec expected. Tie-break: none. The large effect is as reported in small samples; small studies tend to overstate effects, so the helps text must not lean on the size. This is the grade I am least sure of, because four independent labs agreeing on direction could be read as B.
Suggested rewording: "Sleeping on a difficult problem, after first working on it, can raise the chance of finding a hidden solution at retest compared with the same time awake; the benefit is confined to hard problems and depends on sleep stage." This would earn **C**, borderline B.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed: four small experiments whose boundary conditions narrow the general claim is C; Wagner et al. (2004) and Sio, Monaghan & Ormerod (2013) have no cached abstract, but their quotes match PubMed (PMIDs 14737168, 23055117).

### Replication
No Many Labs / RRR of time pressure and divergent thinking specifically was found. Byron, Khazanchi & Nazarian's (2010) meta-analysis of 76 experimental studies is the largest available synthesis and itself resolves prior "equivocal evidence" into a moderated (curvilinear) pattern rather than a simple negative one — which is the key finding for grading.

### Backfire evidence
- Baer & Oldham (2006, above) and Byron, Khazanchi & Nazarian (2010, above) **both independently report a curvilinear (inverted-U), not simply negative, relationship** between time/evaluative pressure and creativity — i.e., some pressure can help, especially for open, well-supported people, and only high pressure clearly hurts. This directly contradicts a flat "time pressure always reduces divergent output" reading of the claim, and is exactly the backfire the brief anticipates ("Is the relationship curvilinear, so that moderate pressure helps? That would contradict the style rule as currently written.") — it does. **The grading agent should treat the current claim wording ("time pressure... reduces... output") as an oversimplification of two independent, moderately-large, high-quality sources that both find an inverted-U instead.**
- Amabile, Hadley & Kramer's (2002) HBR piece (unverified, see above) is reported in secondary literature to have found a "time-pressure hangover" (creativity drops on high-pressure days and stays lower the following day even under lower pressure) but this could not be independently verified this session.

### Grade
**C** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: The read evidence does not support a flat negative relation. Byron, Khazanchi & Nazarian (2010, meta-analysis, abstract read) find a *curvilinear* relation for evaluative stress, where low evaluative contexts "increased creative performance", and a linearly negative one for uncontrollability. They conclude that "stressors' effect on creativity is more complex than previously assumed". The abstract does not isolate time pressure. Baer & Oldham (2006, abstract read; field study, n = 170) find an inverted U for time pressure, but only among open employees who had support for creativity. On re-grade, Moreau & Dahl (2005, cited under constraint-relaxation; abstract via OpenAlex) add that input constraints helped only when people were "not under significant time constraints". That is consistent with high time pressure hurting. Amabile et al. (2002) is unverified and not used. Mixed results that partly contradict the claim give C, as the spec expected. Tie-break: none; there is only one meta-analysis, so tie-break 3 does not apply.
Suggested rewording: "High time pressure during idea generation tends to lower creative output; low to moderate pressure need not, and may help some people." This would earn **C**.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed: the cached Byron et al. and Baer & Oldham abstracts report curvilinear relations that partly contradict the flat claim, which is C.
