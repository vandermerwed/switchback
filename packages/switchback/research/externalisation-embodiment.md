## unfulfilled-goal-offload
**Claim:** Writing down unfinished tasks and concerns, with at least a next step for each, reduces their intrusion into attention during later work. Listing them without any plan does not.
**Construct:** the cognitive effects of unfulfilled goals; offload by plan-making.
**Used by:** brain-dump (LB)
**Load-bearing:** yes

### Sources
| Cite | DOI | Year | Type | Design | n | Effect | Finding (quoted from abstract) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Masicampo & Baumeister (2011) | 10.1037/a0024192 | 2011 | experiment | 5+ within-study experiments (reading task, lexical accessibility, anagram) | not stated in abstract | not reported as standardized effect size | "Unfulfilled goals persist in the mind... Unfinished goals caused intrusive thoughts during an unrelated reading task (Studies 1 and 5B), high mental accessibility of goal-related words (Studies 2 and 3), and poor performance on an unrelated anagram task (Study 4). Allowing participants to formulate specific plans for their unfulfilled goals eliminated the various activation and interference effects. Reduction of the effects was mediated by the earnestness of participants' plans." (full text fetched, PDF) |
| Ramirez & Beilock (2011), *Science* | 10.1126/science.1199427 | 2011 | experiment | 2 lab + 2 randomized field experiments; study 1 n=20 | not given as standardized d/g in the abstract | "The intervention, a brief expressive writing assignment that occurred immediately before taking an important test, significantly improved students' exam scores, especially for students habitually anxious about test taking. Simply writing about one's worries before a high-stakes exam can boost test scores." (abstract box, full text fetched) |
| Scullin, Krueger, Ballard, Pruett & Bliwise (2018) | 10.1037/xge0000374 | 2018 | experiment (polysomnography RCT) | n=57 healthy young adults | not independently confirmed (only press summary read) | "They were randomly assigned to write about tasks that they needed to remember to complete the next few days (to-do list) or about tasks they had completed the previous few days (completed list). Participants in the to-do list condition fell asleep significantly faster than those in the completed-list condition. The more specifically participants wrote their to-do list, the faster they subsequently fell asleep, whereas the opposite trend was observed when participants wrote about completed activities." (abstract via OpenAlex) |

### Replication
No replication of Masicampo & Baumeister (2011) itself was found (searched directly); none found for Ramirez & Beilock either within this session's remaining budget. Not "none found (exhaustive)" — flag for a follow-up search if this claim needs an A grade.

### Backfire evidence
The Scullin et al. (2018) result is a partial complication for the claim's second sentence ("listing them without any plan does not [reduce intrusion]"): a bare to-do list (not a plan with a stated next step) still produced a measurable benefit — faster sleep onset — and the benefit scaled with how *specific* the list was, not with whether it contained plans. This is a different outcome measure (sleep onset latency vs. intrusive-thought/anagram interference) than Masicampo & Baumeister used, so it does not directly contradict their no-plan condition, but it does mean "bare listing has no effect" cannot be asserted as a general finding across outcomes. The original Masicampo & Baumeister no-plan condition itself is the source's own internal backfire: goal activation persisted in that condition, motivating the claim's plan-making requirement.

### Grade
**C** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: Re-graded 2026-09-25 against the abstract cache. The core claim still rests on one multi-study paper from one lab. Masicampo & Baumeister (2011) was read in full text: unfinished goals caused intrusions and interference, and "formulat[ing] specific plans ... eliminated" them. The no-plan condition supplies the claim's second sentence. Ramirez & Beilock (2011, abstract read) is a neighbouring construct: writing about worries before an exam raised scores. Scullin et al. (2018) now counts (abstract via OpenAlex). Writing a to-do list, with no plan, made people fall asleep faster than writing about completed tasks, and more specific lists worked better. The outcome differs from Masicampo & Baumeister's, but it complicates the claim's "listing them without any plan does not" clause: a specific list without plans helped something. No replication of any of the three was found. Few studies, mostly from different constructs, is C. Tie-break: none. The adversarial pass should check whether Ramirez & Beilock, a 2011 Science paper, was in the Social Science Replication Project. **Design note:** brain-dump's printed prompt asks for no next step. Masicampo & Baumeister make the plan the active ingredient, and Scullin et al. suggest that specificity matters, so the page would be better grounded with a next-step column.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed: one lab's multi-study paper plus two neighbouring constructs is C, and all three abstracts match the cache. Whether Ramirez & Beilock (2011) failed a replication was not searched (C claims are not searched); a failure would not lower C, because the claim rests on Masicampo & Baumeister.

## free-listing-salience
**Claim:** When people freely list what they know or think about a domain, the items they list earlier and more often are the ones most salient to them.
**Construct:** free-listing salience (output order and frequency).
**Used by:** brain-dump (LB)
**Load-bearing:** yes

### Sources
| Cite | DOI | Year | Type | Design | n | Effect | Finding (quoted from abstract) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bousfield & Barclay (1950) | 10.1037/h0059019 | 1950 | experiment | associative-response listing task | not stated | n/a | Foundational demonstration of the relationship between output order and frequency of restricted associative responses; read via secondary description only, not the original text. |
| Romney & D'Andrade (1964) | 10.1525/aa.1964.66.3.02a00870 | 1964 | theory/method (componential analysis) | kin-term elicitation | n/a | n/a | Early use of free-listing/sorting to surface a domain's cognitive structure (English kin terms); read via secondary description, not original text. |
| Smith (1993) | 10.1177/1525822x9300500301 | 1993 | practice (method) | methodological note | n/a | n/a | Defines the weighted free-list salience index, combining an item's frequency across lists and its mean rank position; read via secondary description. |
| Quinlan (2005) | 10.1177/1525822x05277460 | 2005 | practice (method) | fieldwork methodology (ethnobotany) | n/a | n/a | "In freelists, informants create an inventory of all the items they know within a given category. Freelists reveal cultural salience and variation in individuals’ topical knowledge. ... This method, however, does not work well with broad topical areas: People tend to omit some items and cluster responses as they unpack mental subcategories." (abstract via OpenAlex) |

### Replication
Not applicable in the RCT/meta-analysis sense — this is a methods literature, not an effect with replications to check. No large pre-registered test of "list-order predicts self-rated salience" specifically was found.

### Backfire evidence
Sousa, Soldati, Monteiro, Araújo & Albuquerque (2016), *PLOS ONE*, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0165838 (verified): "A typical assumption of this approach is that individual lists reflect a subset of total knowledge and that the first items listed are the most culturally important. ... We find that individuals have a tendency to recall information about medicinal plants used during the preceding year and that the recalled plants were also the most important plants during this period." (abstract via OpenAlex; **cache note:** the abstract shows that recall favours recently used items, which in this sample were also the most important ones. It does not itself show that recency is confounded with importance.) Read together with a broader critique surfaced in this session's search (unattributed synthesis, not itself a single verified citation): free-list salience indices are argued to conflate **accessibility/recency** with **importance** — items recalled first may simply be the most recently or frequently *encountered*, not the most important. This is directly relevant to brain-dump's read-back instruction ("recurrence is salience"): the read-back may be detecting rumination/recency rather than true priority. Flag as the claim's chief boundary condition.

### Grade
**C** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: Re-graded 2026-09-25 against the abstract cache. Sousa et al. (2016, abstract via OpenAlex) is the one empirical test of the method's assumption "that the first items listed are the most culturally important". Recall favoured plants used in the preceding year, and "the recalled plants were also the most important plants during this period". That partly supports the claim, with a recency qualifier: what gets listed tracks what is recent, which in this sample was also what mattered. Quinlan (2005, abstract via OpenAlex) is methods guidance: "freelists reveal cultural salience", but people "omit some items and cluster responses" on broad topics. Bousfield & Barclay (1950), Romney & D'Andrade (1964) and Smith (1993) have no cached abstract and stay unread. One observational study plus methods guidance is C: few studies, and the claim's link between *order* and salience is untested. Tie-break: none; tie-break 6 no longer applies, because an empirical study now bears on the claim. Transfer is adjacent: medicinal-plant knowledge is not one's own current concerns, and a brain-dump read-back can mistake a recent loop for a priority.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed: Sousa et al. (2016) directly tests whether free lists "indicate the most important items", with a recency qualifier, and its title ("biased by memory") makes the result mixed; that is C.

### Replication
The meta-analyses (Nesbit & Adesope 2006; Schroeder et al. 2018) broadly agree that concept mapping beats passive study, but Karpicke & Blunt (2011) and its conceptual replication (Lechuga et al. 2015) show retrieval practice consistently beats concept mapping on the *comparisons node-map competes against in this collection* (§6.2 tie-break 3 relevant: two meta-analyses agree with each other but disagree in spirit with the Karpicke/Lechuga line, which used a different comparison condition — concept mapping vs. retrieval, not vs. passive reading).

### Backfire evidence
Not independently sourced this session for the "time cost" / "dense maps photograph poorly" / "vague link labels" questions in the brief; flagged as unresolved.
