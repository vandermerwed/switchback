## testing-effect
**Claim:** Retrieving studied material from memory (for example, writing down everything you can recall with the book closed) improves retention at a delay of days or more, compared with spending the same time restudying it.
**Construct:** retrieval practice (testing effect) · **Used by:** free-recall (LB, transfer **direct**) · Series (LB, transfer **adjacent**)
**Load-bearing:** yes

**Read status (grading):** All four sources count. Rowland (2014) and Pan & Rickard (2018) were read verbatim at source. Roediger & Karpicke (2006) and Adesope et al. ### Replication
No Many Labs or RRR project targeting the testing effect specifically was found (searched: general knowledge of the Many Labs 1–5 and RRR project lists; none names test-enhanced learning as its target effect). The effect's robustness instead rests on the convergence of the four independent meta-analyses above, spanning 1975–2017 source studies.

### Backfire evidence
- Roediger & Karpicke 2006 itself: at a 5-minute (near-immediate) final test, repeated restudy beat repeated testing; the testing advantage appeared only at the delayed test. This bounds free-recall's design: the benefit is a delayed-retention effect, not an immediate-performance one.
- Pan & Rickard 2018: transfer of testing is **weakest** for untested material seen during initial study and for problems involving worked examples, and their publication-bias-corrected models show the transfer intercept is "often indicating no positive transfer when none of the aforementioned moderators are present" — i.e., transfer is conditional, not a free-standing near-zero-cost bonus. This is directly relevant to tie-break rule 2 (§6.2) **for the transfer question specifically**, though it does not touch the core (non-transfer) testing-vs-restudy retention effect, which Rowland 2014 grades robustly.
- Flag for the grading step: Rowland 2014 reports I² = 84.35% (>75%), which trips tie-break rule 4 (§6.2, "high unexplained heterogeneity → at most B, unless a reported moderator matches our conditions of use"). Rowland does report a moderator — initial test type (recall vs. recognition) — that plausibly matches free-recall's own design (open-book-closed free recall), which the grading agent should weigh against the heterogeneity cap.

### Grade
**A** · transfer **direct (free-recall) · adjacent (Series)** · effect **moderate**
Rationale: Four meta-analyses bear on the claim. The pooled testing-vs-restudy estimate is g = 0.50 [0.42, 0.58] (Rowland 2014), and Adesope et al. (2017) find that "practice tests are more beneficial for learning than restudying and all other comparison conditions". No failed large replication was found, so tie-break 1 does not apply. Tie-break 2 does not apply either. Pan & Rickard's near-zero PET-PEESE intercept concerns *transfer* to untested or rearranged items. It does not concern retention of the tested material, which is what this claim states. Tie-break 4 is triggered by Rowland's I² = 84.35. But Rowland reports a moderator that matches our conditions of use, "initial recall tests yielding larger testing benefits than recognition tests", and free-recall is a recall test. So the grade is taken on that subgroup and stays A. The notes do not record that subgroup's own effect size or heterogeneity; that is the point for the adversarial pass to check. The benefit is delayed: at a 5-minute test, restudy won (Roediger & Karpicke 2006), and the claim's "days or more" already builds that in. Tie-break: none capped the grade (4 was considered, and the moderator exemption applied). Tie-break: none.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed: the bias check the grader lacked clears tie-break 2. Yang et al. 2021 (222 classroom studies) find PEESE g = 0.475, 3PSM g = 0.461 and trim-and-fill 0 missing, against a raw g = 0.499 (10.1037/bul0000309). Rowland's retention-interval moderator matches the claim's "days or more" (at least 1 day: g = 0.69 [0.56, 0.81], k = 56). The grader's recall-vs-recognition exemption misreads Rowland's Table 1, though. Initial free-recall tests gave g = 0.29 [0.07, 0.52] (k = 36), no larger than recognition, and Yang's free-recall quizzes gave g = 0.238. Free-recall's own format therefore sits at the small end of "moderate".

## retrieval-feedback
**Claim:** Checking recall against the source right after retrieving it increases the retention benefit and corrects errors that would otherwise persist. Without feedback, errors produced during recall tend to be retained.
**Construct:** feedback in test-enhanced learning; self-scoring accuracy · **Used by:** free-recall (LB: the second-colour check pass)
**Load-bearing:** yes

### Sources

On the other hand, feedback after correct responses made little difference either immediately or at a delay, regardless of whether the subject was confident in the response." **Read status (grading):** All four sources count. Pashler et al. (2005) and Butler & Roediger (2008) were read verbatim at source; Butler & Roediger has no cached abstract, but its quote was fetched from the full-text PDF. Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2008) and Dunlosky et al. (2011) now quote their cached abstracts. The cache dates Dunlosky et al. 2010, which is the online-first year.

### Replication
No large pre-registered replication of feedback timing (immediate vs. delayed) was found (searched).

### Backfire evidence
- Dunlosky et al. 2011 (above): when learners self-score their own recall against a standard, they are systematically overconfident unless forced to judge idea-unit by idea-unit rather than globally — directly relevant to free-recall's design, which relies on the person self-administering the check pass. This supports building the check as an idea-unit-level comparison rather than a global "did I get it right?" judgment.
- Pashler et al. 2005 (above): feedback's benefit is concentrated on **correcting errors**; it does little for already-correct recall. This means free-recall's check pass functions mainly as error-correction, not as a general retention booster on top of testing itself — worth naming explicitly in the item's `helps` text.

### Grade
**B** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: Three independent experiments were read, and they agree. Correct-answer feedback after an error raised one-week retention by 494% (Pashler et al. 2005, N = 258). Feedback on a multiple-choice test raised correct answers and cut lure intrusions on a delayed test (Butler & Roediger 2008). Feedback doubled the retention of low-confidence correct answers (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger 2008). The feedback moderators in Rowland (2014) and Adesope et al. (2017) were not extracted, so there is no meta-analytic estimate and the claim cannot reach A; no failed replication was found. The mechanism is narrower than "increases the retention benefit" suggests: feedback after correct responses "made little difference" (Pashler et al.), so the check pass works by correcting errors and low-confidence correct answers. Transfer is adjacent, and self-scoring is the weak point: students grading their own recall are overconfident, even for commission errors, unless they judge idea unit by idea unit (Dunlosky et al. 2011). The check pass should therefore be laid out idea by idea. Effect is n/a: there is no standardised pooled estimate, and the 494% applies to error items only. Tie-break: none.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed: three consistent read experiments support B, and Rowland's full-text feedback moderator (g = 0.73 with feedback vs 0.39 without; 10.1037/a0037559) agrees with the claim's direction; nothing found meets a tie-break.

**Read status (grading):** Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001) is **unread**: it was described only via a later paper, and it has no cached abstract. Metcalfe & Finn (2011) and Metcalfe (2017) were read verbatim at source. Butler, Fazio & Marsh (2011) was read verbatim from the Duke Scholars record, although it has no cached abstract. The cache dates Metcalfe 2016, the online-first year.

### Replication
No Many Labs/RRR-style replication was found. Butler, Fazio & Marsh 2011 functions as a direct extension/replication of Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001's paradigm at a 1-week delay, and confirms the core effect persists but weakens.

### Backfire evidence
Butler, Fazio & Marsh 2011 (above) is itself the backfire/boundary finding the brief asked for: high-confidence errors, if the correction is later forgotten, are **more** likely than low-confidence errors to reappear at delay. So hypercorrection's benefit is not permanent — it depends on the correction itself being retained, which raises the same forgetting curve the effect otherwise ameliorates.

### Grade
**B** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: Without the seminal paper, the grade rests on Metcalfe & Finn (2011, three experiments) and Butler, Fazio & Marsh (2011), backed by Metcalfe's (2017) Annual Review: "Errors committed with high confidence are corrected more readily than low-confidence errors." That is several consistent experiments from at least two labs, with no meta-analysis found, so the grade is B. Butler et al. set the boundary. The effect held at one week but was weaker, and "high-confidence errors were more likely than low-confidence errors to be reproduced on the delayed test". The benefit therefore depends on the correction being revisited. Transfer is adjacent, because the studies used general-knowledge questions. This is a supporting claim and does not move free-recall's grade. Tie-break: none.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed: several consistent experiments from at least two labs, with no meta-analysis and no failed replication found, meet B; this is a supporting claim.

The Carpenter & Toftness abstract confirms the gap-fill's corrected reading: no cost to non-prequestioned content was found in the video study.

### Replication
No Many Labs/RRR replication was found; Pan & Carpenter 2023 is the most current synthesis and post-dates the seminal studies by well over a decade, functioning as an informal large-scale replication check across many labs' individual studies.

### Backfire evidence
- **Gap-fill correction (2026-09-25):** Carpenter & Toftness 2017 (above) was previously read as evidence of a selective-processing cost. On closer reading (still via WebSearch synthesis, not a verbatim primary quote — see the row above), the paper's own video experiment found **no** such cost: prequestioning helped both prequestioned and non-prequestioned content. The selective-processing risk it discusses is attributed to *prior reading-based studies*, not to its own video findings. This weakens, rather than supports, the backfire case for pretesting/prequestioning specifically as applied to video or passage material — though the risk may still hold for reading tasks per those prior studies, which were not independently traced this session.
- Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009's own introduction explicitly weighs the pretesting effect against **errorless-learning theory**, which predicts the opposite (that unsuccessful retrieval should impede, not help, learning) — the brief's "does instruction-first ever win" question is the live theoretical fault line here, not a settled boundary condition.

### Grade
**B** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: Four sources agree that attempting questions first helps later retention of the questioned material, even when the first answers are wrong: Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009, 6 experiments), Richland et al. (2009, 5 experiments), Carpenter & Toftness (2017) and Pan & Carpenter's (2023) review. No meta-analysis was sourced, and the review says the size of the enhancement "may vary due to differences in procedure or how learning is assessed". That makes it B. The cached abstract settles the gap-fill's open question on Carpenter & Toftness. Its video study found benefits for both prequestioned and non-prequestioned content. The risk of impairing non-prequestioned material is attributed to earlier *reading* studies, which were not sourced. The claim concerns the prequestioned material, so this bounds the `cued` variant's use but does not lower the grade. Tie-break: none.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed: four consistent sources meet B. A search found no failed large replication. Newer syntheses bound the effect to prequestioned material, which matches the claim as written. This is a supporting claim.

### Extraction (per the brief) — updated
**Lead correction:** claims.md's brief names Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli 2013 as the "Evidence" source for the number of sessions. The actual primary data on session-count and diminishing returns comes from **Rawson & Dunlosky 2011** (Experiment 3, N=335, tested 1–5 relearning sessions), as reviewed and quoted directly in **Rawson & Dunlosky 2012** (fetched in full this session, above) — a different paper from the one claims.md names. Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli 2013 and the Higham et al. 2022 replication (see below) are course-embedded field studies that used **three** sessions by design choice, not because they demonstrated three is where returns plateau.

**What the evidence actually says:** Retention increases from 1→2→3→4→5 relearning sessions with a shrinking but still-positive increment at every step (34%→46%→54%→60%→63% correct recall at the 1-month test, per Rawson & Dunlosky 2011's data as summarized in the 2012 review). The authors' own account of "diminishing returns" is about **time cost**, not benefit: "completing more relearning sessions produces diminishing incremental costs (i.e., in time spent studying), because the speed of relearning accelerates across sessions" (Rawson & Dunlosky 2012, General Discussion). The only point where they observe near-zero *marginal benefit* is between the fourth and fifth session, not the third. Their own recommendation — "at least three relearning sessions" — is an evidence-based floor chosen because the cost/benefit ratio is favourable by that point, explicitly **not** a claim that benefits stop growing after three, and they refuse to call any number a "magic number." A separate, more recent paper (Rawson, Dunlosky & Janes 2020) found the successive-relearning advantage is much weaker for procedural material (probability problem-solving) than for the verbal/definitional material used in the session-count studies above — a genuine boundary condition on the whole construct, distinct from the diminishing-returns question.

**Bottom line for grading:** claims.md's "returns diminish after about three relearning sessions" is not well supported as stated. The literature supports "three sessions is a reasonable, evidence-based minimum, chosen for good cost/benefit reasons, with further sessions continuing to help modestly" — a materially different and weaker claim. The grading agent should treat Series' "3 sessions" as a defensible design choice (D-adjacent, closer to a parameter grounded loosely in evidence) rather than as an empirically-derived point of maximal return.

### Backfire evidence
Rawson, Dunlosky & Janes 2020 (above) is itself the clearest backfire/boundary evidence located this session: successive relearning's benefit shrinks and is inconsistent for procedural (mathematical) material relative to the verbal/definitional material most of this literature uses — directly relevant since Series applies the technique to "mixed personal material," not course key-terms. The brief's other backfire questions (do learners drop items too early; does targeting only the gaps starve weakly-held items) remain open — no source located this session tests them directly. Bahrick 1979 (the brief's other seminal lead, decades-long relearning studies) was not located/verified this session due to time; it is cited within the Dunlosky et al. 2023 guide as the theoretical basis for why forgetting necessitates relearning at all, but not for the specific session-count question.

### Grade
**C** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: The claim has two parts. The first, that relearning to criterion across spaced sessions gives durable retention, rests on one lab's programme read through two reviews by its authors: Rawson & Dunlosky (2012), which summarises a study of 335 students given 1–5 relearning sessions, and the 2023 guide. The second part is contradicted by the only data read. Retention at one month kept rising through all five sessions: three sessions gave a 60% relative gain over one, and five gave 86%. The only near-flat step was from four to five. The authors decline to name a "magic number"; they recommend "at least three" as a cost–benefit floor, and the guide says "research has not yet systematically explored 'how much is enough'". The claim as written therefore earns C: few studies, from one group, and the plateau is contradicted. The "dropping" part also needs care. In Kornell & Bjork (2008; see distributed-practice), being allowed to drop flashcards "had small but consistently negative effects on learning". Tie-break: none.

Suggested rewording: Relearning items to a criterion across several spaced sessions gives durable retention, and each added session helps by a shrinking amount through at least five sessions; three is a sensible minimum, not the point where gains stop. (C, adjacent; B once Rawson et al. 2013 and the Higham et al. 2022 replication are read)

Parameter recommendation: keep 3 sessions as a design choice, labelled a minimum, not the point of greatest return. Never print or document that returns diminish after three. Keep "each round targets the previous round's gaps", but revise it so that correctly recalled items stay in later rounds: targeting should add prompts for gaps, not drop what was recalled.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed: C stands. The notes rest on one group's programme, whose data contradict the three-session plateau (C claim, no search run).

Sinha & Kapur 2021's meta-analysis is itself the field's replication-level synthesis: it aggregates 53 studies and finds the PS-I/productive-failure advantage is **not** nulled by publication-bias correction (the bias-corrected estimate is larger, not smaller, than the raw estimate — an unusual direction worth flagging for the grading step, since tie-break rule 2 (§6.2) concerns bias-corrected estimates that go to zero or reverse, which is not what happened here). ### Backfire evidence
Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006 is the field's central counter-argument and directly answers the brief's backfire question: minimally guided, discovery-style, or generate-before-instruction designs can underperform guided instruction, particularly for **novices** and for material with **high element interactivity** (many interacting parts that cannot be inferred without prior schemas). Sinha & Kapur 2021's own moderator analysis is consistent with a bounded version of this concern: PS-I's advantage did *not* hold for younger learners (2nd–5th graders) or for domain-general skills, where instruction-first (I-PS) was favored instead. For question-queue, this bounds the claim: deferring the AI's answer until after the person has attempted a question works best when the person has enough relevant background to attempt productively; on genuinely novel, high-complexity material with no foothold, deferring may cost more than it teaches — a live risk the brief itself names ("A deferred question that blocks the work can stall the sitting").

### Replication
No large pre-registered RRR of transfer-appropriate processing itself was found. The clearest "failed-to-replicate the naive expectation" result is Sala & Gobet's own finding that far-transfer effects collapse toward zero once active control groups are used — this functions as boundary evidence against overclaiming transfer, not a replication of Morris et al.'s specific effect.

### Backfire evidence
Brady's contextual-interference meta-analysis is a genuine *tension* with the claim as stated: **varying** practice conditions (high contextual interference) that do **not** resemble the eventual test context reliably improve retention and transfer (g ≈ .38, larger in basic research), relative to blocked practice that matches conditions trial-to-trial. So "make practice resemble the target" needs the caveat the brief anticipated: matching *cues and demanded actions* to the target (transfer-appropriate processing) is not the same axis as matching *trial-to-trial conditions* (contextual interference/variability of practice), and the second sometimes cuts the other way. Smith & Vela's meta-analysis also bounds the claim: context effects shrink or vanish once encoding/retrieval encourage non-contextual (introspective) processing — i.e., practice-audit's benefit should be framed as "resemble the *processing demands*," not "resemble every incidental circumstance."

### Grade
**B** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: With the seminal transfer-appropriate processing study unread, the claim rests on syntheses of neighbouring constructs. Sala & Gobet (2017) conclude that "far transfer of learning rarely occurs", and Barnett & Ceci (2002) find that transfer depends on conditions. Smith & Vela (2001) find context effects reliable, but reduced when non-contextual cues carry learning and test. In this family, Pan & Rickard (2018) report that "response congruency" between practice and test strongly influences transfer of test-enhanced learning, which directly supports "resemble the target's required actions". Against one reading of the claim, Brady's (2004) contextual-interference meta-analysis (.38 overall; .19 in applied research) shows that varying practice conditions often helps. Tie-break 3 was considered and does not apply. These meta-analyses measure different axes, processing match and trial-to-trial conditions, and do not disagree about one effect. The grade is B on convergent meta-analytic support, with the core study unread. The page's four-question audit format is CFAR craft and untested. Tie-break: none.

Suggested rewording: Practice transfers best when it demands the same kind of retrieval or response as the target task, and transfer to a different skill is rare; what should match is the processing demanded, not the surface conditions, which are often better varied. (B, adjacent)
