### Replication
**Gap-fill update (2026-09-25) — resolved.** Sparrow, Liu & Wegner (2011) was indeed among the studies targeted for replication under the Social Sciences Replication Project (Camerer et al. 2018, *Nature Human Behaviour*, DOI 10.1038/s41562-018-0399-z, re-verified via Crossref this session: title/authors/year match). The SSRP replication attempt targeted the paper's "Google Stroop effect" (the priming/interference sub-finding, not the "lower recall of information itself" sub-finding), led by Holzmeister & Camerer, and **it failed to replicate**. Confirmed via a directly-fetched source — Hesselmann (2020), *PeerJ*, DOI 10.7717/peerj.10325 (verified via Crossref this session: title "No conclusive evidence that difficult general knowledge questions cause a 'Google Stroop effect'. A replication study", author Hesselmann, year 2020) — whose abstract, read directly via PMC (PMC7651475), states: "A much-cited study published in *Science* in 2011 provided first behavioral evidence for such changes in human cognition... the 'Google Stroop effect' could not be replicated in two recent replication attempts as part of a large replicability project. After the failed replication was published in 2018, the first author of the original study pointed out some problems with the design of the failed replication." The same fetched source further states, describing the SSRP attempt specifically: "this replication... did not show a significant effect despite adequate statistical power." Hesselmann (2020) is itself a **second, independent** replication attempt (incorporating the original author's methodological objections to the SSRP version) and also found "no conclusive evidence in favor of the notion that the concept of the Internet or internet access... is automatically activated when participants are faced with hard trivia questions." **This is a confirmed failed large replication of the priming/Stroop sub-finding specifically** (Camerer et al.'s SSRP replications used samples ~5x the original on average). Per §6.2 tie-break 1, this caps that sub-finding at C. Note the scope: this bears on the "primed to think about computers" mechanism, not directly on the separate "lower recall of the information itself, better recall of where to find it" transactive-memory finding from the same paper, which was not the subject of either replication attempt found this session — that sub-finding (closer to what player-aid/dashboard/question-queue actually lean on) remains unreplicated either way, a genuine gap.

### Backfire evidence
Built into the claim itself (weaker memory for offloaded content — Sparrow et al. 2011; Storm & Stone 2015's effect is conditional on the saving process being reliable). Meyerhoff et al. (2021) adds a caution that offloading tendencies don't generalize well across tasks/individuals. The brief's question about "keeping the store up to date becoming a second job" was not resolved with a source in this session — a gap, not a confirmed finding. **Gap-fill note (2026-09-25):** the now-confirmed failed replication of the Stroop/priming sub-finding is itself a backfire-relevant caution for how much weight cognitive-offload's Sparrow et al. citation should carry — the grading pass should distinguish the (replicated-elsewhere, more behavioural) transactive-memory/recall-location finding from the (failed-replication) automatic-priming finding when writing the rationale.

### Grade
**B** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: Several independent experiments point the same way. Gilbert (2015; four experiments, N = 1,196) found that setting external reminders improved the fulfilment of delayed intentions; Storm & Stone (2015; three experiments) found that saving one file improved memory for the next, but only when saving was reliable; and Sparrow, Liu & Wegner (2011) found lower recall of information people expected to be able to look up, which is the claim's cost half. Risko & Gilbert (2016) review the construct. No meta-analysis, so B. Tie-break 1 was checked and does not apply: the failed SSRP and Hesselmann (2020) replications target Sparrow et al.'s "Google Stroop" priming sub-finding, not offloading's performance benefit or the recall cost, which stays unreplicated either way. Meyerhoff et al. (2021) caution that offloading habits do not generalise across tasks, and the "keeping the store current becomes a second job" backfire is unsourced. Only abstracts were read.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed B: an independent lab found both halves across three experiments (N = 172 each): offloading supports "immediate performance" and "offloading did have detrimental effects on memory without such a goal" (Grinschgl, Papenmeier & Meyerhoff 2021; 10.1177/17470218211008060); I found no failed replication of the performance or recall-cost findings, and no meta-analysis, so the grade stays B.

Urbach et al. (2014) is explicitly a large-scale (>200,000 procedures) natural-experiment attempt to replicate the Haynes et al. Per §6.2 tie-break 1, a failed large replication caps the grade at C for the surgical-checklist-specific evidence, though the broader "job aid reduces omissions" construct (Reason 2002; aviation/manufacturing practice) is not solely dependent on the surgical literature.

### Backfire evidence
The brief's question about checklist complacency (ticking without checking) was not resolved with a quoted source in this session — a gap. Hales & Pronovost (2006) notes slow adoption in medicine relative to other fields, which is adjacent context, not a direct backfire finding.

**C** · transfer **analogical** · effect **n/a**

### Adversarial pass

### Replication
Ghibellini & Meier (2025) is the meta-analysis the brief anticipated ("lead: Ghibellini & Meier, a recent meta-analysis") — I can now confirm the year (2025) and venue (*Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* 12), which the brief did not specify. **This is good news for the claim as Longhand actually uses it**: the claim as stated in claims.md is about *resumption*, not memory recall, and the meta-analysis directly supports a resumption tendency ("Ovsiankina effect represents a general tendency") while explicitly finding *no* memory (Zeigarnik) advantage. The spec's expectation of "C/D" may be pessimistic for the resumption half of the construct specifically, though the memory-recall half is confirmed null.

### Backfire evidence
Masicampo & Baumeister (2011) shows unfinished/open tasks do cause intrusive thoughts — consistent with the brief's flagged tension with brain-dump's offload claim — but also shows this is eliminated by *making a specific plan*, which is functionally what a resumption cue (next claim) provides. This suggests stop-mid-task's benefit may depend on pairing it with a resumption cue rather than standing alone.

### Grade
**C** · transfer **analogical** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: The claim joins a supported half to an untested one. Ghibellini & Meier's (2025) meta-analysis (abstract read) finds "a general tendency to resume tasks" that were interrupted (the Ovsiankina effect) and no memory advantage for them (the Zeigarnik effect), which supports the likelihood of resuming. Nothing read tests ease of resumption, stopping mid-sentence against stopping at a natural break, or gaps of days, and the abstract gives no pooled effect. Masicampo & Baumeister (2011) show that open tasks cause intrusive thoughts until a specific plan is made. Partial support from one meta-analysis read at abstract level, with the claim's specific terms untested, gives C. Tie-break: none. For the cover and Sitting, the line is better paired with a written next step (resumption-cues, B) than left as "stop mid-sentence" alone.
Suggested rewording: "People tend to resume tasks that were interrupted before completion; unfinished tasks are not better remembered." This would earn **B** (one meta-analysis, abstract only, no pooled effect given).

### Adversarial pass
confirmed C: Ghibellini & Meier 2025's cached abstract supports only a general tendency to resume, and nothing read tests ease of resumption, stopping mid-sentence, or gaps of days.

### Replication
Not a single-effect replication design, but four independent studies (Altmann & Trafton 2002; Trafton et al. 2003; Monk et al. 2008; Hodgetts & Jones 2006) from overlapping but distinct labs converge on the same goal-activation model, which is a reasonable proxy for convergent validity even without a formal RRR.

### Backfire evidence
Monk et al. (2008) directly answers the brief's "do cues decay in value over long gaps?" question in the affirmative for interruptions up to ~1 minute (resumption cost scales with duration and demand) — but Longhand's gaps are days to weeks, several orders of magnitude longer than anything tested here. This is the clearest **transfer gap** in this batch: the mechanism is well-supported at lab timescales (seconds to a minute), and Longhand is relying on it holding at timescales the literature has not tested. Hodgetts & Jones (2006) shows that altering the cue after it's set actively *impedes* resumption relative to no cue at all — a caution that a stale or ambiguous cue could be worse than none.

### Grade
**B** · transfer **analogical** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: Three read sources from overlapping labs support the claim at lab timescales. Hodgetts & Jones (2006) found that encoding retrieval cues before an interruption cut resumption time, and that altering those cues afterwards impeded it; Monk, Trafton & Boehm-Davis (2008; three experiments) found resumption time grows with interruption length and demand, as the memory-for-goals model predicts; Altmann & Trafton (2002) is that model, tested by simulation. Trafton et al. (2003) has no cached abstract and stays unread. Several consistent experiments and no meta-analysis gives B. Tie-break: none. Transfer is analogical for Sitting, Ritual and the cover, whose gaps are days, against lab gaps of seconds to a minute, and adjacent for the dashboard's in-session use; the "errors" half of the claim is not directly shown in the read abstracts.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed B: a meta-analysis of 33 laboratory experiments found interruption interventions "significantly increased primary task accuracy (SMD = 1.03 ...) and reduced resumption lag (SMD = -0.51 ...)", with effects varying by type of intervention (Guo, Chen, Xie & Or 2021; 10.1016/j.apergo.2021.103506); it pools cues with other interventions and does not report a cue-only estimate, so it cannot lift the grade, and I found no failed replication.

## task-switching
**Claim:** Switching between tasks carries a measurable time and accuracy cost compared with staying on one task. Doing one kind of thinking at a time avoids that cost.

### Replication
Rogers & Monsell (1995) and Monsell's (2003) review represent one of the most heavily replicated basic-cognition effects in the literature (switch costs are robust and textbook-standard); the applied extension to real-world "media multitasking," however, **fails to replicate reliably**: Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein's own 2 replication attempts plus meta-analysis of 39 effect sizes found the association "turned nonsignificant after correction for small-study effects."

### Backfire evidence
This is the key nuance for Sitting's design rationale: the basic lab switch-cost effect (millisecond-scale, simple two-task alternation) is robust, but the applied, real-world extension the brief's "media multitaskers" lead (Ophir, Nass & Wagner 2009, not independently re-sourced this pass — cited only via Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein's own review of it) does **not** hold up under replication. Sitting's "one move per page" design is closer to the robust lab paradigm (discrete, coarse-grained moves) than to the applied media-multitasking claim, but the transfer is still, per the brief, "analogical" — lab switch costs are measured in milliseconds on trivial classification tasks, and Sitting's moves take minutes. Leroy's (2009) attention-residue finding is the one source that bridges toward the coarser, minutes-scale claim Sitting actually needs, but was only read via secondary paraphrase this pass. The brief's own tension (does breaking off one task help incubation?) was not independently resolved here; it is cross-referenced to creativity-fixation/incubation-effect, sourced in a different batch (CF).

### Grade
**B** · transfer **analogical** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: Rogers & Monsell (1995, now read via its cached abstract) found a substantial switch cost across six experiments that preparation reduced but did not remove ("a large asymptotic reaction time (RT) cost remained"). Monsell (2003) and Leroy (2009, attention residue, the one source at the minutes scale Sitting needs) have no cached abstract and stay unread. Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein (2017) found the media-multitasking–distractibility link non-significant after correction for small-study effects, but that is an individual-difference claim, not switch cost, so tie-break 2 does not reach this claim. Consistent multi-experiment evidence from one lab, with no read meta-analysis, gives B at its lower edge. Tie-break: none. Transfer analogical: millisecond costs on digit and letter classification do not show that moving between thinking pages costs anything. Supporting claim only.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed B (lower edge): Rogers & Monsell's cached abstract gives a switch cost that preparation reduced but did not remove, I found no failed replication of switch costs themselves, and the null media-multitasking result (Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein 2017) concerns a different individual-difference claim.
