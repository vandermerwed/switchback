# Mining: BestSelf (Self Planner / Self Journal)

**Sources:** *Self Planner Instructions 2021* (guidebook, read in full, 11 pages); *SelfPlanner_2021* (the undated planner itself — Project Plan, 6-Month Bucket List, BestSelf Benchmark, Monthly Planner + Habit Tracker, Monthly Reflection, Weekly Planner, Weekly Canvas dot-grid pages, Quarterly Reflection & Planning — sampled by distinct page type); *SJV5.2_Goodnotes* — identified as the guidebook + templates for BestSelf's other product, the **Self Journal** (v5.2), a 13‑week daily/weekly execution journal built around an "Outcome Goal → Progress Milestones → Critical Drivers" roadmap, daily Gratitude/MIT/timeline/metrics pages, and weekly planning/review — sampled by distinct page type.

## 1. Source summary

BestSelf is a commercial productivity-planner line (Self Planner, undated monthly/weekly organiser; Self Journal, a 13-week goal-execution journal) sold with a guidebook of "why" copy. Its philosophy is maximalist and totalizing: fill every white-space minute ("zero-based scheduling"), track everything, hit a 13-week cycle hard, and treat undone squares as failure ("no unaccounted time"). This is close to the opposite of Longhand's "an unfinished workbook is a worked workbook." Evidence citation in the guidebook is thin — one paragraph name-drops Locke & Latham on goal-setting and "studies indicate" gratitude effects, with no other sourcing — so almost everything here is presented as practitioner wisdom, not tested claims; a few of its moves nonetheless map onto real, well-studied constructs that Longhand doesn't yet cover. The product's real differentiator from Longhand is scale (a whole life, indefinitely) and completeness (no page left blank), not any single move; most individual techniques are either already in Longhand under other names, or are day-management mechanics that sit outside Longhand's per-problem workbook model.

## 3. Per-candidate notes (medium and high value)

### Wheel-of-life multi-domain rating — new preset of `scoresheet`

**What it is.** BestSelf's "BestSelf Benchmark" page asks the person to rate 6 fixed life domains (Health & Fitness, Work & Career, Social & Relationships, Mindfulness, Finance, Home & Family) each 1–10, write a line of free text under each, then total and average all six. It's re-administered later (BestSelf ties it to the 13-week cycle) so the person can compare averages over time.

**On paper.** A page prints 5–7 labelled domain rows (Longhand's own taxonomy, not BestSelf's named categories), each with a 1–10 scale to circle and 1–2 lines to write on, plus a total/average box. It reuses `scoresheet`'s rating mechanic (a felt quantity, marked by hand) but applies it in parallel across several named categories on one page, which `scoresheet` today doesn't do (it rates one quantity, twice).

**What the agent reads back.** The individual domain numbers and lines, plus the average as a single number to track between administrations — not to optimize, but to flag which domain moved most since last time and read out the free-text line under it.

**Backfire.** Averaging masks the domain that actually matters: a single very low score (say, health) can be swamped by five good ones, and the average looks "fine." Re-rating is subject to the same prior-state-recall bias as `scoresheet` (§4.8 in claims.md) — being shown or half-remembering the earlier number can anchor the new one. Domains aren't equally weighted for any given person, and BestSelf's own six-category list is arbitrary.

**Research leads (to verify).** This is not just a coaching worksheet — it closely resembles a real psychometric instrument: the **Personal Wellbeing Index** (Cummins, R. A. and the International Wellbeing Group; index manual editions from the early-to-mid 2000s) rates 7 fixed domains 0–10 and averages them into a subjective-wellbeing score, and is validated against Diener's broader subjective-well-being tradition (Diener, E., 1984, "Subjective well-being," *Psychological Bulletin*). The originating "Wheel of Life" coaching exercise itself has no verifiable single inventor (often attributed to Paul J. Meyer / Success Motivation Institute, 1960s, unverified) and should print as "traditional coaching tool," but the *grading claim* should point at the PWI/domain-satisfaction literature, not at the coaching provenance. Grade prior **B**; transfer **adjacent** (a validated 7-item instrument administered by researchers differs from a self-designed 6–7 category page re-administered quarterly by the same person alone).

**Maps to claims.md?** No existing claim covers multi-domain simultaneous rating; it would need a new claim, most naturally filed under `planning-self-regulation` alongside `progress-monitoring` and `prior-state-recall` (both of which it inherits as supporting claims from `scoresheet`), or as a new small family if the lead prefers to keep periodic wellbeing assessment distinct from within-sitting self-monitoring.

### Same-day task-duration forecast — variant of `forecast`

**What it is.** SJV5.2's daily page gives each of the day's top-3 tasks a row of small circles, one per 30 minutes, that the person fills in *before* starting to estimate how long the task will take, then works the actual task against a same-day hour-by-hour timeline. By evening, the person can see estimate vs. actual for several tasks from that single day.

**On paper.** This slots into the existing `forecast` page's shape (a written, dated, falsifiable prior, resolved later) but shrinks the resolution horizon from weeks/months to hours, and multiplies the number of forecasts per sitting from one to several (one per task). The dated, pre-committed circle count is exactly forecast's "tamper-evident record" mechanic, just applied to duration instead of an external event.

**What the agent reads back.** How many circles were filled vs. how many 30-minute blocks the task actually consumed on the timeline; over several sittings, whether the person is a systematic under- or over-estimator, which is exactly the calibration signal `forecast`'s existing claims (`calibration-feedback`, `hindsight-record`) are built on.

**Backfire.** The roster already flags forecast's real weakness: "a few scored forecasts gives noisy feedback." A duration-forecast variant used several times per Sitting directly answers that — many small, fast-resolving trials instead of one slow one — but inherits the planning fallacy's own backfire: people often stay optimistic even after seeing the gap, unless they explicitly unpack the task into steps first (which is `timeline`'s job, so the two pair well). There's also a reactivity risk: once a person knows estimates are being tracked, they may pad them defensively rather than estimate honestly.

**Research leads.** The construct is much closer to a **direct** test than event forecasting is: Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994) and the planning-fallacy literature they founded studied exactly this — people predicting their own task-completion times and comparing to actual — so this variant's transfer is **direct to adjacent** where the base `forecast` page is only adjacent. Reuse forecast's existing calibration-training leads (Lichtenstein & Fischhoff 1980; Mellers et al. 2014) for the "does feedback improve calibration" half, and Buehler, Griffin & Peetz (2010, review) for whether unpacking or feedback alone fixes the bias. Grade prior **B/C**.

**Bigger idea.** This pushes on Gate 1's restriction of `forecast` to "styles with a return (Ritual, Series)." A same-day duration variant resolves within the same Sitting (at the evening return / commit stage), so it arguably qualifies without waiting for a Ritual or Series structure. Recommend the lead consider a same-day carve-out to that restriction rather than a blanket loosening (see §4).

### Countdown token — new preset of `tokens`

**What it is.** SJV5.2 has two related devices: a streak calendar of X's marking consecutive days of action toward the 13-week goal, and a row of dots on the weekly review page that fill in as "weeks remaining" ticks down from 13 to 0.

**On paper.** A preset of the `tokens` piece: instead of tokens representing a budget spent across competing options (the base component's use), a fixed set of tokens (one per week or per session in a Series/Ritual arc) is physically placed, coloured, or removed as the cycle progresses, so the remaining count is always visible on the desk.

**What the agent reads back.** How many tokens remain vs. the plan, and whether the person's own effort or check-in scores visibly rise as the remaining count shrinks (the pattern the underlying research predicts).

**Backfire.** The effect that motivates this is asymmetric: it predicts a *motivation dip* early in a long cycle when the remaining distance still looks large, not just a boost near the end. A bare countdown for a 13-week arc could therefore discourage early effort unless paired with nearer-term milestones (which is exactly what `timeline`/the milestone-roadmap preset already does). There's also a risk that an externally visible countdown crowds out intrinsic motivation for the goal itself.

**Research leads.** This is a genuinely well-evidenced, currently unclaimed mechanism for Longhand: the **goal-gradient effect** — Hull (1932) originated it in animal learning; Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng (2006, *Journal of Marketing Research*, "The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected") ran field experiments on real loyalty-card progress (visible remaining stamps), which is close to a direct analogy for a visible remaining-token count; Bonezzi, Brendl & De Angelis (2011) and Cheema & Bagchi (2011) extend it to perceived distance and visualized goals. Grade prior **B**, transfer **adjacent** (loyalty cards vs. a self-tracked personal cycle).

**Maps to claims.md?** No existing family covers this. Recommend adding it as a new supporting/load-bearing claim under `planning-self-regulation` (it sits naturally beside `progress-monitoring` and `timeboxing`), rather than opening a new family for one claim.

### Gratitude line — variant/option of `check-in`

**What it is.** Both BestSelf products open the day with "three things you're grateful for" or "one thing with three reasons why," explicitly named in the guidebook as a positive-psychology technique.

**On paper.** One additional fixed row on the `check-in` page (Ritual's single recurring page), not a new component — Ritual's budget is one page, and `check-in`'s design already mixes fixed rows with free-write.

**What the agent reads back.** What recurs across returns (the same free-listing-salience logic already used for `brain-dump`: what's named often is what's most present), and whether the line goes empty or rote over many returns (a signal the ritual has become a chore, which the roster already treats as a known Ritual backfire).

**Backfire.** Frequency matters: some gratitude-journaling studies find *weekly* listing outperforms *daily* (variety/habituation effects — Lyubomirsky, Sheldon & Schkade 2005 on hedonic adaptation to repeated positive activities), which cuts in Longhand's favour since Ritual defaults to weekly, not daily. It can also read as forced or performative if not genuinely felt (Sansone & Sansone's review flags this), and risks becoming a box-ticking exercise exactly like any other recurring page.

**Research leads.** Emmons & McCullough (2003, "Counting blessings versus burdens," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) is the seminal study; Seligman et al. (2005) tested "three good things" as a positive-psychology intervention directly; look for a meta-analysis (lead: Cregg & Cheavens 2021; Davis et al. 2016 systematic review) to set the grade. Grade prior **B**, transfer **adjacent** (studies mostly use dedicated multi-week gratitude-journal protocols; here it's one row on a weekly page).

**Maps to claims.md?** New claim needed; best housed in `perspective-emotion`, which already carries the collection's other affect/self-perception claims (`expressive-writing`, `self-distancing`).

### Wins-and-lessons prompts — variant/option of `check-in`

**What it is.** BestSelf's weekly and monthly reviews consistently ask for "3 big wins," a "highlight," and "lessons learned," alongside the free-write.

**On paper.** Suggested fixed-row content for `check-in`'s free-write area (or its `helps` text), rather than a new page — it structures what the person writes about on the existing Ritual page.

**What the agent reads back.** Recurring wins (salience across returns, same mechanism as brain-dump's read-back) and whether "lessons learned" repeat verbatim across returns, which would itself be a signal worth surfacing (the same lesson keeps needing to be relearned).

**Backfire.** A forced "3 wins" quota can manufacture wins that aren't genuine, and "lessons learned" sections are notoriously prone to being written and never revisited (the same critique levelled at `five-whys` and RCA practice generally).

**Research leads.** The mechanism is **savoring** — Bryant & Veroff (2007, *Savoring: A New Model of Positive Experience*) is the theoretical source; Quoidbach and colleagues have reviewed savoring interventions. Grade prior **C**, transfer **adjacent**; this is thinner evidence than the gratitude line and closer to practice craft, and should be labelled as such if adopted.

### Milestone roadmap — preset of `timeline`

**What it is.** SJV5.2's "13-Week Outcome Goal" page draws a mountain profile: a SMART outcome goal at the summit, 2–3 "progress milestones" as base camps along the ascent, and "critical drivers" (recurring actions) that get you from camp to camp.

**On paper.** This is `timeline`'s existing move (order, "what must be true first") with a mountain graphic and a second block for recurring actions per milestone. It fails the distinct-move test against `timeline` (ordering milestones) and `scoresheet`'s `running` variant (tracking recurring critical-driver actions) — nothing here is a new move, only a new preset skin and a two-column layout combining the two.

**Backfire and grade.** Inherits `timeline`'s task-unpacking claim (adjacent, expected C/D) with no preset-specific addition; the mountain graphic itself is pure design choice (D, not a claim).

**Verdict.** Admit as a low-effort preset (a `timeline` preset named for a goal-with-milestones use, keeping the graphic as a design flourish), but it adds no new evidence and shouldn't be over-sold in the grading report.

### Three-state habit mark — variant of `scoresheet` (`running`)

**What it is.** BestSelf's habit tracker uses three distinct marks — missed, partial, completed — rather than a plain check/blank binary.

**On paper.** A small variant to `scoresheet`'s `running` mark: instead of one symbol, three, still self-scored and still read back the same way.

**Backfire.** Finer granularity opens more room to rationalize ("that was basically a completion") — the same Goodhart-style gaming risk `scoresheet` already carries, just with an extra degree of freedom.

**Grade.** No new claim; it's a design choice (D) riding on `scoresheet`'s existing `progress-monitoring` claim.

## 4. Bigger ideas

- **A new supporting-claim family for affect-positive content.** Longhand's `perspective-emotion` family already houses expressive-writing and self-distancing; recommend it also absorb gratitude and savoring claims (both above) rather than opening a ninth family for two related, modest-effect-size claims.
- **Loosen `forecast`'s "styles with a return" restriction for a same-day variant.** The duration-forecast candidate resolves within one Sitting (at the evening return/commit step), so it plausibly satisfies "a style that schedules a return" without needing Ritual or Series. This is a concrete, scoped change to test against Gate 1's existing restriction, not a wholesale reversal of it.
- **Ritual cadence could gain a longer option.** BestSelf's quarterly rhythm (13-week cycle, quarterly Benchmark re-administration) suggests `ritual-cadence` (currently a weekly-default parameter) could explicitly support an occasional longer-period return for multi-domain instruments like wheel-of-life, without inventing a whole new style.
- **A cover/coaching contrast worth stating explicitly.** BestSelf's stated philosophy — "leave ZERO white space," schedule the entire day — is close to the opposite of Longhand's "an unfinished workbook is a worked workbook." Worth a coaching line (or an explanation-doc note) that names this on purpose: Longhand is deliberately not a day-management system and won't grow toward one, even as it borrows individual well-evidenced moves (gratitude, calibration, goal-gradient) from planners that are.
- **"Don't multitask" is real, unclaimed support for an existing principle, not a new page.** Task-switching costs are a robust literature (adjacent to, but distinct from, the already-noted-and-contested ego-depletion caution in claims.md §4.6). It could be cited as supporting evidence for collection principle 3 ("one move per page") or Sitting's mono-tasking framing, but doesn't need its own component.

## 5. Recommendations for the lead

1. **Admit the wheel-of-life preset of `scoresheet`.** It's the strongest find here: a real validated instrument (Personal Wellbeing Index / domain-satisfaction literature) backs a technique Longhand currently has no equivalent for, and it's cheap to build as a preset rather than a new component.
2. **Admit the same-day duration-forecast variant of `forecast`, and reconsider the return-restriction for it specifically.** It directly fixes forecast's stated weakness (too few, too-slow-resolving forecasts) with a mechanism that has more direct research support than the base page's general-event forecasting.
3. **Admit the countdown-token preset of `tokens`.** Cheap (reuses an existing piece), and grounds a new, well-evidenced motivational claim (goal-gradient effect) that nothing in Longhand currently covers — but pair it with milestone markers so it doesn't demotivate early in a long cycle.
4. **Add the gratitude line to `check-in` as an optional row, weekly by default.** Meta-analytically supported, nearly free to add to Ritual's single page, and the "weekly beats daily" nuance in the literature actually favours Longhand's existing default cadence.
5. **Treat wins-and-lessons and the milestone-roadmap graphic as low-cost, low-evidence polish, not priorities.** Both are reasonable content/preset additions to pages Longhand already has, but neither brings new grounding worth spending sourcing budget on ahead of items 1–4.
