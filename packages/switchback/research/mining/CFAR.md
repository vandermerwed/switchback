# Mining: CFAR Handbook (2021-01)

**Source:** *CFAR Participant Handbook*, January 2021 edition (Center for Applied Rationality), 249 pages. Read in full, in 20-page chunks, sampling repetitive reference/glossary material at the end.

## 1. Source summary

The CFAR Handbook is a workshop companion, not a stand-alone course: it explicitly says it "is not designed to be a stand-alone guide" and assumes the reader has already worked the exercises in person. Each of its ~30 named techniques ("classes") opens with a self-assigned **epistemic status** (Preliminary/tentative, Mixed, Anecdotally strong, Firm, Established and confirmed) before any content — an unusually honest, graded-evidence posture that maps directly onto Longhand's own A–D grading and its "grade honestly" principle. Philosophically it treats rationality as "whole-brain": System 1 (the "inner simulator") and System 2 are both first-class tools, most techniques exist to get System 1's tacit judgements into words (or vice versa), and several classes explicitly warn against over-applying a technique past the point it's useful ("eat the instructions," "adjust your seat"). Most individual techniques are either genuinely CFAR-native inventions with little or no formal research behind them (labelled as such by CFAR itself), or are CFAR's own packaging of a well-studied academic construct (implementation intentions, mental contrasting, prospective hindsight) under a house name. The handbook is unusually generous with primary citations in its own "Further Resources" sections, which is a good head start for sourcing.

## 3. Per-candidate notes (medium and high value)

### Goal Factoring — new component

**What it is.** A named algorithm (p.62): choose a costly or puzzling recurring action; explicitly "prepare to accept all worlds" (release your attachment to any particular answer, including keeping the action); factor the action into every distinct goal it serves (not just the stated one — status, identity, and habit are named as easy-to-miss subgoals); check the list for completeness with a "button test" (would you push a button that delivered all those goals with none of the costs?); brainstorm replacement actions one goal at a time, then recombine; and reality-check the resulting plan (the handbook explicitly cross-references Murphyjitsu here).

**On paper.** A page with: the chosen action; a neutrality line ("I am equally willing to find that I should keep doing this or stop"); a node-map-style cluster of goals branching off a central bubble for the action, each with a short "would I push a button?" tick; a brainstorm block for replacement means, one goal at a time; and a closing reality-check line. This differs from `node-map` (relations between concepts) and `options-criteria` (scoring given alternatives) in both direction and purpose: it starts from one entrenched behavior and works outward to its hidden functions, rather than starting from a menu of options.

**What the agent reads back.** The goal list (especially any goal the person didn't expect to write down), whether the button test produced hesitation (a sign the list is incomplete), and whichever replacement action the person actually commits to — which should hand off to `commit`'s if-then block rather than duplicate it.

**Backfire.** CFAR's own text implies two: settling for a plausible-sounding goal list that stops short of the real drivers (identity/status goals are named as the easy ones to miss), and generating replacement actions that are individually "possible" but not "Good, or Practical, or Sane" without a reality check — the handbook's own case study spends several paragraphs on options the person never seriously considers acting on.

**Research leads (to verify).** No direct test of the exercise itself is likely to exist; the closest genuine academic parallels are (a) **goal systems theory**'s equifinality/multifinality framework — Kruglanski, A. W. et al. (2002), "A theory of goal systems," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, which formally treats one means serving multiple goals and one goal servable by multiple means; and (b) **functional behavioral assessment** in applied behavior analysis — O'Neill, R. E. et al. (1997), *Functional Assessment and Program Development for Problem Behavior*, which identifies the function(s) a problem behavior serves and designs a functionally-equivalent replacement, a strikingly close structural analogue. Also check Sheldon, K. M. & Kasser, T. (1995), "Coherence and congruence," *JPSP* (cited by CFAR itself in this section's further-resources, p.63) on alignment between lower- and higher-level goals. Grade prior **C/D**, transfer **analogical**.

**Maps to claims.md?** No existing claim. Recommend a new item in `planning-self-regulation` (joins commit, woop, timeline) with a claim id like `planning-self-regulation/goal-decomposition`, distinct from `decision-diagnosis/decomposed-judgement` (which scores given options, not the hidden functions of one entrenched behavior).

### Aversion Factoring — variant of Goal Factoring

**What it is.** The same "LEGO principle" reductionism, run in the opposite direction: instead of asking what goals an action serves, it decomposes an *avoided* activity into its concrete component sensations and circumstances (p.211's running example splits "running is bad" into nine separately-judged parts, from "shins and knees aching" to "other people looking at me and judging me"), gives each part a verdict, and only then designs fixes — changing external circumstances for some parts, and exposure/reframing for others. A closing "assessing aversions" step explicitly warns that not every aversion is worth curing (p.216: some aversions are "correctly avoid[ed]").

**On paper.** This reuses Goal Factoring's shape (choose → factor into parts → causal-graph the parts and their fixes → reality-check) with two differences that justify treating it as a variant rather than a straight preset: the factoring targets sub-experiences of one activity rather than the multiple goals of one behavior, and each part gets an explicit **verdict** column (roughly: fine / not worth fixing / worth fixing) before any solution is proposed — a deliberate brake against "factoring every flinch," which the source calls out as a waste of resources.

**What the agent reads back.** Which parts got a "worth fixing" verdict, whether the proposed fix is external (a concrete purchase/habit change) or internal (a reframe/graduated exposure — which should route to CoZE), and whether the person actually piloted the fix at the next return.

**Backfire.** Explicitly stated in-source: some aversions protect you from things you correctly don't want to do (the cars-on-the-freeway counterexample), so a factoring habit that always finds a "fix" risks eroding a genuinely useful aversion; and a badly-timed cost-benefit framing ("if I didn't feel bad about it, it wouldn't be bad") could be used to talk oneself into tolerating a real harm.

**Research leads.** As with Goal Factoring, the reductionist frame itself is unlikely to have direct evidence, but the individual *fixes* it routes to are well-studied: **cognitive-behavioral therapy for anxiety disorders** broadly (Norton, P. J. & Price, E. P. (2007), "A meta-analytic review of cognitive-behavioral treatment outcome across the anxiety disorders," *Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease*, cited by CFAR itself here) and **graduated exposure** for the "address internal factors" half. Grade prior **D** for the factoring move itself (anecdotally strong per CFAR, no formal research), but the fixes it routes to can inherit CoZE's stronger grade.

**Maps to claims.md?** Shares `planning-self-regulation/goal-decomposition` as a supporting claim with Goal Factoring; its verdict step and exposure-adjacent fixes should also draw on whatever claim CoZE gets (below).

### Comfort Zone Expansion (CoZE) — new component

**What it is.** A five-step algorithm (p.121–129, condensed at p.222 from Frame-by-Frame Debugging's own cross-reference): choose an experience you're averse to but plausibly want; explicitly prepare to accept both possible worlds (the experiment goes well, or it doesn't, and either is acceptable information); design a small, genuinely safe version of the experience; actually go do it; and afterward "digest" what happened, deciding whether to escalate, repeat, or stop. CFAR frames it as related to exposure therapy but "meaningfully different in that it does not take for granted that a given aversion is inappropriate" (per the glossary definition, p.234) — the goal is a *more accurate* set of anticipations, not a categorically reduced aversion.

**On paper.** A page in three blocks: a pre-experiment block (the aversion, the small safe experiment design, and a written "I accept both outcomes" line so the framing can't be quietly abandoned mid-experiment); a gap for the actual in-world action (this is the one place in Longhand where the "page" mostly just holds space around something that happens off-paper); and a post-return digest block (what happened, how it felt, continue/repeat/stop). This resembles `commit`'s if-then shape and `forecast`'s predict-then-score shape, but is distinct from both: it isn't predicting an external event (forecast) and it isn't closing a decision into an action (commit) — it's designing and logging a **deliberately small, real-world probe** of a felt aversion, which neither existing page does.

**What the agent reads back.** Whether the pre-committed "accept both worlds" line was actually written before the outcome, what the digest records happened vs. what was feared, and the continue/repeat/stop decision — feeding the next round's experiment size.

**Backfire.** CFAR names two explicitly: pushing into an experience you'll regret (framed against exposure therapy's use for aversions that genuinely should be reduced, vs. some that shouldn't), and running the technique on autopilot until you've "fully factor[ed] every flinch," which wastes resources on aversions not worth the effort.

**Research leads.** This is one of the better-evidenced candidates in the whole handbook, because the underlying mechanism is core, mainstream clinical practice rather than a CFAR invention: **behavioral experiments** in cognitive therapy — Bennett-Levy, J. et al. (2004), *Oxford Guide to Behavioural Experiments in Cognitive Therapy* — and the **inhibitory learning model of exposure** — Craske, M. G. et al. (2014), "Maximizing exposure therapy," *Behaviour Research and Therapy*. **Self-efficacy theory** (Bandura, A. (1977), "Self-efficacy," *Psychological Review* — graduated mastery experiences build self-efficacy) is a second strong lead. Grade prior **B/C**, transfer **adjacent** (clinical exposure protocols are usually therapist-guided and target diagnosed anxiety; here it's self-designed and self-administered for an everyday aversion).

**Maps to claims.md?** No existing claim. Recommend a new item, most naturally alongside `planning-self-regulation` or as the seed of a small new family (it would pair well with Aversion Factoring's "worth fixing" fixes); claim id something like `planning-self-regulation/graduated-self-experiment`.

### Internal Double Crux — new component

**What it is.** CFAR's solo adaptation of its (two-person) Double Crux algorithm: operationalize a felt internal conflict — usually a "should" ("I should exercise more, but I don't want to") — as two named "sides," each representing a genuine part of the person rather than a straw-manned one; apply a "good faith principle" to both (assume each side wants something legitimate); seek the crux of the disagreement between them (the belief that, if changed, would resolve the conflict); and test candidate resolutions by imagining living them out and checking for lingering hesitation ("resonance").

**On paper.** Two named columns (labelled by the person, e.g. "the part that wants to exercise" / "the part that's exhausted at 6am"), each populated with that side's stated reasons in its own words; a shared middle area for candidate cruxes (the belief that would settle it); and a closing if-then line for whichever resolution resonates, cross-referencing `commit`'s block. This is distinct from `perspective-swap` (which takes the view of another actual or hypothetical *person*) and from `assumption-audit` (which lists premises of a *plan*, not sides of a felt internal conflict).

**What the agent reads back.** The two sides' stated positions, the candidate crux, and whether the final if-then line reads as resolved (both sides represented, no hedging language) or as a forced System-2 compromise that likely won't hold — the source explicitly warns that a resolution has to satisfy both sides' underlying wants, not paper over one of them.

**Backfire.** In-source: can get lost or derailed mid-dialogue; one side can feel unsafe to think about honestly (identity-threatening material); attending to one side more than the other produces "tunnel vision"; and a resolution that only convinces System 2 without addressing System 1's actual objection will not hold up in practice.

**Research leads.** No direct test is likely for the specific two-column method. Leads worth checking: **intrapersonal goal conflict** — Emmons, R. A. & King, L. A. (1988), "Conflict among personal strivings," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* (goal conflict predicts negative affect and somatic symptoms — this grounds *why* resolving felt internal conflict matters, without testing the resolution method itself); and, more speculatively, **Internal Family Systems** as a "parts" framework — the handbook's own glossary calls IFS "a controversial school of psychotherapy" (p.240), which the lead should read as CFAR's own caution not to over-claim here. Grade prior **D**, transfer **analogical**.

**Maps to claims.md?** No existing claim; recommend a new item, most naturally in `perspective-emotion` (goal-conflict/parts material sits closer to that family's existing self-distancing and perspective-taking claims than to decision-diagnosis).

### Bucket Errors — new component

**What it is.** CFAR frames this (p.80, epistemic status: Mixed) as an applied case of Kahneman's well-established **attribute substitution / question substitution**: a person forms a single mental "bucket" (e.g., "am I a good writer?") that silently absorbs many genuinely distinct questions, so that new information gets sorted into the bucket's single verdict rather than answered on its own terms — producing either flinching defensiveness (when a bucket is overloaded with identity-relevant stakes) or false reassurance (when unrelated good news gets miscredited to a threatened bucket).

**On paper.** A page that starts from a noticed flinch or defensive reaction to some piece of feedback or evidence; asks the person to list the several genuinely distinct questions that might be bundled inside the single reaction (e.g., "is my writing good," "do people take me seriously," "was this one piece rushed"); and re-sorts a small set of pieces of evidence against the separated questions rather than the single bucket. This differs from `assumption-audit` (which lists a plan's premises) and `five-whys` (linear causal descent): it's about **untangling a conflated identity-relevant judgement into its separable component questions**, which nothing else in Longhand does.

**What the agent reads back.** The separated questions, which piece of evidence was originally driving the flinch, and whether re-sorting it against the narrower question changes how threatening it feels.

**Backfire.** In-source, both directions are named as failure modes elsewhere in the handbook's broader "Understanding Shoulds" material: too many buckets prevents seeing real patterns across experiences, while too few (the default problem) causes flinching from any evidence that touches an overloaded bucket.

**Research leads.** The core mechanism is genuinely well-established: Kahneman, D. & Frederick, S. (2002), "Representativeness revisited: Attribute substitution in intuitive judgment," in *Heuristics and Biases* (Gilovich, Griffin & Kahneman, eds.); Kahneman, D. (2003), "A perspective on judgment and choice," *American Psychologist* (both already cited elsewhere in the handbook). The defensive/identity-threat half of the mechanism is best grounded in **motivated reasoning** (Kunda, Z. (1990), "The case for motivated reasoning," *Psychological Bulletin*) and **self-affirmation theory**, which specifically studies reduced defensive processing of identity-threatening information (Sherman, D. K. & Cohen, G. L. (2006), "The psychology of self-defense," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*; Cohen, G. L. et al. (2000), *Journal of Applied Social Psychology*). Grade prior **B** for the underlying substitution effect, **C/D** for the bucket-splitting technique itself; transfer **analogical**.

**Maps to claims.md?** No existing claim. Recommend a new item in `decision-diagnosis` (joins assumption-audit, forecast), claim id `decision-diagnosis/question-substitution`, with a supporting claim on defensive processing.

### Frame-by-Frame Debugging — new component

**What it is.** A six-step diagnostic (p.221–222): pick a recurring "bug"; describe one recent, concrete instance with causal detail (or, failing memory, a characteristic "parable" instance); pinpoint exactly where that instance diverged from the preferred path; zoom to the *exact frame* — the specific trigger-action link — where intervention would need to happen; check whether an alternative action even occurred to you at that moment (distinguishing a **forgetting-type** problem, routed to TAPs/systemization/CoZE/Murphyjitsu, from a **motivation-type** problem, routed to Goal Factoring/Aversion Factoring/Internal Double Crux); and reality-check the resulting fix by imagining it firing across a whole week.

**On paper.** A page built like a short storyboard/timeline: a concrete-instance narrative box, a marked divergence point, a forced yes/no "did an alternative occur to you?" branch, and a routing line to whichever other Longhand page fits the diagnosed bug type. This is a genuinely distinct move from `five-whys` (open-ended linear "why" descent, with the roster's own admitted backfire being an arbitrary stopping point and a tendency to end in blaming a person): Frame-by-Frame requires one *concrete* recent instance rather than an abstracted symptom, targets a single *frame* rather than a chain of "why," and adds the forgetting/motivation triage that routes to a specific next tool — five-whys has no equivalent branch and no built-in defense against its own blame backfire.

**What the agent reads back.** The concrete instance, the marked divergence frame, the forgetting/motivation classification, and which other Longhand page (if any) the person was routed to and actually used next.

**Backfire.** Not stated explicitly for this technique in-source, but two are implicit: substituting a fabricated "parable" instance for a real memory risks diagnosing a bug that never quite happened that way; and misclassifying a motivation-type problem as forgetting-type (or vice versa) sends the person to the wrong follow-up tool.

**Research leads.** The closest strong academic analogue is **chain analysis** in Dialectical Behavior Therapy — Linehan, M. M. (1993), *Cognitive-Behavioral Treatment of Borderline Personality Disorder* — a clinical technique for tracing a problem behavior's precipitating link-by-link chain to find intervention points, evaluated as part of DBT's broader RCT evidence base rather than in isolation. The "fix" side inherits `planning-self-regulation/implementation-intentions` (already A/B-graded) for forgetting-type bugs. Grade prior **C/D** for the diagnostic frame itself, transfer **analogical** (DBT chain analysis is a clinical technique for serious behavioral dysregulation; here it's applied to everyday recurring annoyances).

**Maps to claims.md?** No existing claim covers the diagnostic triage step. Recommend a new item in `decision-diagnosis`, positioned explicitly as addressing five-whys' own admitted backfire (arbitrary stopping, blaming a person) — the grading report should note this contrast.

### Mundanification — new component

**What it is.** A short technique (p.225, one page in-source): when a possible outcome feels too catastrophic to think about clearly — the handbook's example is failing a class or getting expelled — deliberately imagine it concretely and in detail rather than flinching away from it. The stated purpose is not to make the outcome good, but to stop it from carrying "infinite weight" in System 1's calculations, which the source explicitly likens to being unable to do arithmetic with an undefined term.

**On paper.** A page with: the feared outcome named plainly; a "what would actually happen, concretely" block (what the next day, week, and month would look like); and a before/after felt-magnitude rating (reusing `scoresheet`'s before-after mechanic). This differs from `pre-mortem` in both target and purpose: pre-mortem imagines *why a specific plan failed*, in order to generate preventable reasons; Mundanification takes an *already-existing dread* (not tied to one plan) and makes it concrete in order to defuse its felt magnitude, with no requirement to generate preventive actions.

**What the agent reads back.** The before/after magnitude ratings, and whether the "concretely, what would happen next" block reads as genuinely specific or stays abstract (a sign the exercise didn't land).

**Backfire.** Not stated explicitly for Mundanification itself, but two are worth naming from the adjacent literature: rehearsing a catastrophe in detail could reinforce rumination rather than defuse it if done without the felt-magnitude check; and making a genuinely unacceptable risk feel "thinkable" and mundane could wrongly normalize tolerating it.

**Research leads.** This candidate has stronger available grounding than most CFAR-native techniques, because "detailed imagination of a feared outcome reduces its felt threat" is close kin to established constructs: **decatastrophizing** in cognitive therapy (Beck, A. T. (1979), *Cognitive Therapy of Depression*); the **affective-forecasting "impact bias"** — people overestimate the intensity and duration of their emotional reaction to feared/negative events (Gilbert, D. T. & Wilson, T. D. (2000), "Miswanting," in *Feeling and Thinking*; Gilbert, D. T. et al. (1998), "Immune neglect," *JPSP*); and **defensive pessimism** as a related coping style (Norem, J. K. & Cantor, N. (1986), "Defensive pessimism," *JPSP*). Grade prior **B/C**, transfer **adjacent**.

**Maps to claims.md?** No existing claim. Recommend a new item in `perspective-emotion`, claim id `perspective-emotion/decatastrophizing`, with impact-bias and defensive-pessimism as supporting leads.

### Turbocharging — new component

**What it is.** A model (p.192–200, epistemic status: Mixed) for auditing whether a chosen *practice method* will actually transfer to a desired *real-world skill*, via four questions: how closely does the practice trigger resemble the real trigger; where they differ, does the practice deliberately vary the trigger to force generalization; how closely does the practice action resemble the real action; where they differ, does the practice deliberately vary the action for flexibility. The source's anecdotes (a math student who takes careful notes but can't do the homework; a police officer who reflexively hands a disarmed weapon back to its owner) all illustrate well-practiced but wrongly-targeted skills — "practice makes permanent," not "practice makes perfect."

**On paper.** A two-column comparison page: the desired real-world trigger/action in one column, the current practice method's trigger/action in the other, with the four audit questions as prompts, and a revise-or-keep decision at the bottom. This is genuinely distinct from Longhand's existing pages: nothing currently asks whether a *practice regime* is actually training the intended skill, as opposed to planning a task (`timeline`), scoring options (`options-criteria`), or explaining a concept (`feynman`/`self-explain`).

**What the agent reads back.** Where the audit found a mismatch (trigger, action, or both), and what revision the person committed to.

**Backfire.** In-source, several vivid failure anecdotes (the aikido student's flinch defenses, the bomb-dogs trained on the wrong tanks, the language student who can conjugate but not converse) all illustrate the core backfire directly: intending to train one skill while actually reinforcing an adjacent, unwanted one.

**Research leads.** This is grounded in genuinely well-studied territory: **transfer-appropriate processing** (Morris, C. D., Bransford, J. D. & Franks, J. J. (1977), "Levels of processing versus transfer appropriate processing," *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*); the classic **identical elements theory of transfer** (Thorndike, E. L. & Woodworth, R. S. (1901), *Psychological Review*); and reviews of **conditions for far transfer** (Barnett, S. M. & Ceci, S. J. (2002), "When and where do we apply what we learn?," *Psychological Bulletin*). Grade prior **B/C**, transfer **adjacent** (well-studied in motor learning and instructional design; the specific four-question audit format is CFAR's own).

**Maps to claims.md?** No existing claim. Recommend a new item in `memory-retrieval` (Longhand's existing learning-and-skill family), claim id `memory-retrieval/practice-specificity`. Especially relevant to the Series style, which is explicitly about learning.

### Murphyjitsu — variant of pre-mortem

**What it is.** CFAR's own named "Inner Sim algorithm" (p.41): select a goal or plan; outline it concretely enough to visualize; check a "surprise-o-meter" (would you actually be shocked if this plan failed?); if not shocked, run "pre-hindsight" (construct a plausible narrative for why it failed, the same prospective-hindsight move as `pre-mortem`); brainstorm preventive actions for each failure reason and visualize taking them; and iterate steps 3–5, since CFAR states each additional cycle roughly halves the remaining odds of failure (an unsupported specific claim the source itself doesn't cite evidence for).

**On paper.** This is `pre-mortem` with two additions: an explicit surprise-o-meter gate (a scale answering "how shocked would I be," which decides whether to continue) and a repeat loop with a preventive-action column that gets re-simulated. Recommend implementing as a variant of `pre-mortem` (e.g. a `bulletproof` variant) rather than a new component — the core move (imagine the plan has already failed; generate reasons) is identical to what `pre-mortem` already does; the addition is a stopping rule and an iterate-with-fixes structure.

**What the agent reads back.** The surprise-o-meter rating at each round, the preventive actions added, and whether a later round still produces genuine surprise or has become rote.

**Backfire.** The source itself flags one candidate mechanism as "rampant speculation" (an unsupported footnote hypothesizing an evolved excuse-generating faculty) and should not be cited as if it were a finding. The genuine, evidence-backed backfire is `pre-mortem`'s existing one: confidence can rise while the plan doesn't actually change, especially across repeated, increasingly rote iterations.

**Research leads.** Inherits `decision-diagnosis/prospective-hindsight` (Mitchell, Russo & Pennington, 1989) and `decision-diagnosis/premortem-overconfidence`. The "each cycle halves the odds" claim has no citation in-source and should be flagged to the lead as an unsupported quantitative claim not to carry into any printed `helps` text.

**Maps to claims.md?** No new claim needed beyond pre-mortem's existing two; add a note that the iterative/surprise-gate structure itself is unevidenced (D) if it's kept as printed guidance.

### The Strategic Level — variant of five-whys

**What it is.** A three-level framework (p.182–183) for responding to a mistake: Level 0 is basic, "ground-level" reaction (pain, regret, self-blame) which the source explicitly says to move past; Level 1 ("tactical") generates a concrete next fix; Level 2 ("strategic") asks what *general way of thinking* would have caught this failure ahead of time, and whether that same thinking pattern would also catch other, dissimilar failures.

**On paper.** As a variant of `five-whys`: instead of an open descent of "why," a fixed three-row structure (what happened / the tactical fix / what thinking pattern would have caught it, and would it generalize) that explicitly guards against `five-whys`' own admitted backfire — a single linear chain that ends in blaming a person.

**What the agent reads back.** The strategic-level answer, and — on a later return — whether the same "way of thinking" answer keeps recurring across unrelated mistakes (a sign it's a real strategic upgrade rather than a one-off patch).

**Backfire.** Not addressed in-source, but worth naming: a "what thinking pattern" question invites an equally arbitrary stopping point to the one `five-whys` already has, just one level more abstract; it can also produce a plausible-sounding but untested strategic story that never gets checked against the next failure.

**Research leads.** The closest well-established academic parallel is **systemic vs. proximate cause analysis in safety science** — Reason, J. (1990), *Human Error*, and the "Swiss cheese model" of systemic failure — and the **just-culture** literature that explicitly separates blame from analysis (Dekker, S. (2007), *Just Culture: Balancing Safety and Accountability*). Grade prior **C**, transfer **analogical** (safety-critical organizational failure analysis vs. one person's everyday mistake).

**Maps to claims.md?** No existing claim; recommend as a supporting note on `decision-diagnosis/root-cause-why-chain` rather than a new claim, specifically citing the blame-avoidance framing as a partial answer to that claim's own backfire question.

### Saving State — style/pacing idea

**What it is.** A short piece of advice (p.178–179): at the end of any working process, deliberately do something — write a phrase, keep a physical object, draw a small image — that captures the *felt sense* of where you landed, distinct from a to-do list of "content," so the state (not just the facts) can be quickly re-invoked later without redoing the work that produced it.

**Why it's cheap and worth doing.** This maps almost exactly onto an existing, already-graded Longhand claim — `attention-load/resumption-cues` (Altmann, J. E. & Trafton, J. G. (2002), "Memory for goals," *Cognitive Science*) — just applied to the *close* of a whole Sitting or Ritual return rather than a mid-task interruption. Recommend adding a one-line "in a phrase, what's true now that wasn't before I started" box to Sitting's and Ritual's closing sequence, feeding directly into `carry.md`'s "what the next round must pick up," as a distinct field from the action items `commit` and `question-queue` already produce.

**What the agent reads back.** The captured handle/phrase itself, carried forward verbatim into the next round's opening as a recall cue.

**Backfire.** Not addressed in-source; worth noting that a stale or rote "state" phrase, repeated unchanged across many returns, would itself be diagnostic (nothing has actually moved).

**Research leads.** No new claim needed — this rides entirely on the existing `attention-load/resumption-cues` claim already in claims.md §6.6.

### Turbocharging, Socratic Ducking, and Resolve Cycles — see also §4 (Bigger ideas)

Socratic Ducking (p.159–160) is coaching guidance, not a printable page: it names a discipline for helping someone else think — offer few direct suggestions, alternate between challenging questions and silent attentiveness, counter vagueness, draw out concrete experience — that is a close, on-the-nose precedent for Longhand's own "asks, not tells" principle and for how `from-paper`'s follow-up questions should be phrased. It has no felt sense of a printable component, so it is listed here as guidance rather than in the per-candidate notes above; see §4 for the concrete recommendation.

Resolve Cycles (p.130–138) is a defined technique ("short, focused bursts of high-effort brainstorming and problem solving, often in series," per the glossary at p.245) but has no fixed worksheet shape of its own in-source — it's closest to a **pacing rule** (a 5-minutes-to-solve / 5-minutes-to-brainstorm-next-actions / act loop) usable as a stuck-task escape hatch inside a Sitting, rather than a new page; see §4.

## 4. Bigger ideas

- **A "how a Sitting closes" addition: Saving State.** Cheap, already covered by an existing graded claim (`attention-load/resumption-cues`), and directly strengthens the `carry.md` mechanism the umbrella design already commits to. Recommend adding this before spending any new sourcing budget on it.
- **Socratic Ducking as explicit style for `from-paper`.** The handbook's description of a good "duck" — few direct suggestions, alternating challenge and silence, countering vagueness, drawing out concrete experience — reads like a ready-made style guide for how `from-paper` phrases its questions back to the person, and for the planned `/how-to/two people` doc in the site's information architecture (§12 of the umbrella design already lists "two people" as a how-to topic). Recommend the lead consider folding this in as explicit phrasing guidance rather than a new claim.
- **A shared small "behavior-change" claim neighborhood.** Goal Factoring, Aversion Factoring, and Comfort Zone Expansion all route into each other in-source (Frame-by-Frame Debugging's own routing table names all three together, p.222) and share a family resemblance (reductionist decomposition → accept-all-worlds neutrality → small real-world test). Recommend the lead consider whether these three want a small dedicated claim neighborhood inside `planning-self-regulation`, rather than being scattered individually, so their shared "accept all worlds" framing can be graded and written up once.
- **A recurring "accept all worlds" framing box.** Goal Factoring, Aversion Factoring, Internal Double Crux, and CoZE all separately instruct the person to genuinely accept either outcome before starting, so the exercise isn't secretly run to justify a conclusion already reached. This could become a small, reusable instructional element (a framing line printed above the working area) shared across whichever of these components are admitted, rather than four separately-worded versions of the same caution.
- **Turbocharging is specifically relevant to the Series style.** Series is Longhand's only style explicitly about learning; a practice-specificity audit page would let a person check, mid-Series, whether their chosen study/practice method for round two actually resembles the skill they're building toward, before spending the next round's budget on it.
- **Overlearning as a Series rule, not a page.** "Deliberately over-apply one technique for several days past first mastery" (p.169) could become an optional Series-style rule — pick one admitted Longhand component and use it repeatedly across a day or week — rather than any new printed content.

## 5. Recommendations for the lead

1. **Admit Comfort Zone Expansion.** The strongest evidence base of anything found here (behavioral experiments, inhibitory-learning exposure, self-efficacy theory are all mainstream, well-cited literatures), it fills a genuine gap (nothing in Longhand currently scaffolds a small real-world behavioral probe), and it pairs naturally with Aversion Factoring's "worth fixing" verdicts.
2. **Admit Goal Factoring and its Aversion Factoring variant together.** Distinct move, cheap to build as one base plus one variant, and directly answers the umbrella design's own stated gap-fill list (§5.5 already names it as a known gap to evaluate).
3. **Admit Frame-by-Frame Debugging as a genuinely new component, explicitly positioned against five-whys' known backfire.** It is the strongest "bug hunting" candidate in the source, and its forgetting/motivation triage plus concrete-instance requirement directly patches the arbitrary-stopping and blame problems the roster already flags for five-whys.
4. **Admit Mundanification, with Negative Visualization as its proactive/habitual variant.** Better-evidenced than most CFAR-native techniques (decatastrophizing, affective-forecasting impact bias, defensive pessimism are all real literatures), cheap to build (reuses scoresheet's before/after mechanic), and distinct in purpose from pre-mortem.
5. **Ship Saving State immediately as a style change, ahead of any new-component sourcing.** It needs no new claim (rides on the existing, already-graded resumption-cues claim), costs one line on two existing styles' closing sequence, and strengthens the multi-session `carry.md` mechanism the umbrella design already committed to.
