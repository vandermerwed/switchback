**Read status (grading):** Clinton (2019) and Calvo-Ferrer (2022) were read verbatim at source. Delgado et al. (2018) and Ackerman & Goldsmith (2011) were recorded from title and metadata only; they now quote their cached abstracts. Kong, Seo & Zhai (2018) is **unread**: it was recorded from its title only and has no cached abstract.

### Replication
Three independent meta-analyses (Delgado et al. 2018, Clinton 2019, Kong, Seo & Zhai 2018) converge on the same direction of effect (paper comprehension advantage, moderated by text type and time pressure), which functions as strong triangulated replication even without a single named RRR/Many-Labs study. No failed large replication of the core comprehension effect was found in this session (searched).

### Backfire evidence
This is the single most important finding for Proof and sheet specifically, because their task is **error-finding in AI drafts**, not comprehension of expository prose: Calvo-Ferrer 2022 found the **opposite** direction of effect for a proofreading/error-detection task — screen-based review detected more errors than paper-based review, especially typographic/non-linguistic ones. This is a genuine boundary condition, not just a smaller effect: the general screen-comprehension-inferiority literature (Delgado, Clinton, Kong et al.) may not transfer to Proof's actual task, which is closer to editing/error-detection than to comprehension-for-learning. This should weigh heavily in grading Proof's `paper-vs-screen` claim, since the brief's own sourcing instruction anticipated exactly this gap ("Search: evidence on proofreading or error detection on paper vs screen specifically") and the one study found argues against, not for, Proof's use of paper for this purpose. Ackerman & Goldsmith 2011's mechanism (self-regulation of study time) also suggests the comprehension effect may be conditional on self-paced study behaviour that doesn't apply to a fixed proofreading task with explicit instructions to find errors.

### Grade
**A** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **small**
Rationale: Two independent meta-analyses were read, and they agree. Clinton (2019; 33 studies, n = 2,799) finds g = −.25 overall, −.32 for expository text and −.04 for narrative. Delgado et al. (2018; 54 studies, 171,055 participants) find g = −0.21, with a larger paper advantage for time-constrained reading and for informational text. No failed large replication or bias-corrected null was recorded. Heterogeneity was not recorded, but the text-genre moderator matches Proof's expository drafts in any case. Both estimates sit just above 0.2, so tie-break 7 is not strictly triggered. The effect is labelled small anyway, and the helps text says so. The "time limits" half is unsettled. Ackerman & Goldsmith (2011) found a screen deficit only when study time was self-regulated, not when it was fixed, which is the opposite pattern to Delgado et al.'s. Transfer to Proof is adjacent at best. Proof's task is finding errors in AI drafts, and the one error-detection study found screen review "más efectivo" for both error types (Calvo-Ferrer 2022). The grade holds for comprehension; it is not support for proofreading on paper. Tie-break: none.

### Adversarial pass
downgraded A→B: tie-break 4 applies. Delgado et al. 2018 report I² = 72.24 for between-participant studies and I² = 89.88 for within-participant studies, which had no moderator analysis (10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003). Their prediction intervals span zero (−.56 to .14, and −.90 to .47), and they note "clearly unexplained variance". The newest meta-analysis, by the same group, finds g = −0.11 for handheld devices, with I² = 94.39 (Salmerón et al. 2024; 10.1037/edu0000830). It also reports that tablet studies varying the time frame "resulted in null effects", so the "under time limits" clause is unsettled too.

**Construct:** automation bias and complacency; cognitive forcing · **Used by:** Proof (LB) · sheet (S)

**Read status (grading):** Parasuraman & Manzey (2010), Lyell & Coiera (2016) and Buçinca et al. (2021) were read verbatim at source. Mosier et al. (1998) and Goddard et al. (2012) were recorded from metadata only; they now quote their cached abstracts. Mosier et al.'s abstract defines omission and commission errors but does not report their rates. It bears mainly on mitigation. The cache dates Goddard et al. 2011, the online-first year.

### Replication
Goddard et al. 2012 and Lyell & Coiera 2016 are two independent systematic reviews (health-informatics-focused) that both find automation bias is a robust, frequently observed phenomenon, functioning as a form of literature-level replication across many primary studies. No large pre-registered failed replication of the core automation-bias phenomenon was found in this session (searched); Lyell & Coiera 2016's contribution is a *refinement* of the conditions under which it occurs (verification complexity/cognitive load, not strictly multitasking) rather than a failure to replicate the phenomenon itself.

### Backfire evidence
Buçinca, Malaya & Gajos 2021 supplies exactly the backfire/mitigation trade-off the brief asked about: cognitive-forcing interventions that make people engage more deliberately with AI output do reduce overreliance, but users rate the designs that work best as the **least** liked, and the benefit was concentrated in people already disposed to effortful thinking (high Need for Cognition) — i.e., a forcing function risks being resented or under-used by exactly the people who most need it. This directly bears on Proof's design: marking/confirmation steps meant to force active review of AI output could be abandoned or rubber-stamped if they are experienced as too onerous, especially by lower-Need-for-Cognition users, unless the friction is calibrated carefully.

### Grade
**B** · transfer **adjacent** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: The phenomenon is well supported. Parasuraman & Manzey (2010) and Lyell & Coiera's (2016) systematic review of 40 studies find omission and commission errors with imperfect aids, in naive and expert users, and in single tasks. Goddard et al. (2012, 74 studies) review how often automation bias occurs and what mediates it, and Buçinca et al. (2021, N = 199) show overreliance on an AI. There is no meta-analysis, and Lyell & Coiera note that "Few studies reported the statistical significance of AB compared to a control condition", so the claim cannot reach A. The mitigation half is thinner and mixed. Cognitive forcing reduced overreliance in one experiment (Buçinca et al.), and Goddard et al. list design and accountability mitigators. Against that, Parasuraman & Manzey say automation bias "cannot be prevented by training or instructions", and in Mosier et al. (1998) "experimentally manipulated accountability demands did not significantly impact performance". The claim is graded B on the phenomenon; the mitigation half is nearer C. Tie-break: none.

Suggested rewording: People reviewing automated or AI output accept its errors at elevated rates, both acting on wrong outputs and missing unflagged problems; training and instructions do not prevent this, while design steps that force evaluation of each output reduced overreliance on AI in one experiment. (B for the first clause; C for the last)

### Adversarial pass
downgraded B→C: the B fits only the claim's first sentence. The second sentence, that forcing interventions reduce overreliance, rests on one experiment (Buçinca et al. 2021, N = 199; 10.1145/3449287). Against it, automation bias "cannot be prevented by training or instructions" (Parasuraman & Manzey 2010; 10.1177/0018720810376055), and Mosier et al.'s accountability manipulation had no effect. That is few studies with mixed results.

## consistent-mapping
**Claim:** Keeping each mark's or colour's meaning constant across contexts leads to faster, more accurate use than mappings that change with context, which provoke mode errors.
**Construct:** consistent vs varied mapping (automaticity); mode errors · **Used by:** proof-actions protocol (LB) · Proof (S) · externalisation-embodiment/colour-language (S)
**Load-bearing:** yes (for proof-actions protocol)

### Sources
| Cite | DOI | Year | Type | Design | n | Effect | Finding (quoted) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Schneider, W. & Shiffrin, R. M., "Controlled and automatic human information processing: I. Detection, search, and attention" | 10.1037/0033-295x.84.1.1 | 1977 | experiment | series of visual/memory search experiments, consistent vs varied stimulus-response mapping | not extracted | consistent mapping produces automatic, fast, effortless detection; varied mapping remains slow, effortful, capacity-limited | Title/authorship/year/pages (1–66, *Psychological Review*) confirmed via Crossref. Co-published with Shiffrin & Schneider 1977 (Part II, DOI 10.1037/0033-295x.84.2.127, same authors/journal/year, also independently confirmed) as the two-part foundational demonstration that consistent mapping — where a given stimulus always calls for the same response — permits automatic processing, while varied mapping (the same stimulus sometimes meaning one thing, sometimes another) never becomes automatic and stays effortful and error-prone. |

The founding stimulus-response-compatibility study: when a stimulus's structure corresponds naturally and consistently to the response it calls for, performance is faster and more accurate than when the mapping is arbitrary or must be reinterpreted per context — the low-level perceptual/motor analogue of the higher-level "keep meanings constant" claim. |

**Read status (grading):** Norman (1981) and Sarter & Woods (1995) were recorded from metadata and description only; they now quote their cached abstracts. Two sources are **unread**. Schneider & Shiffrin (1977), Parts I and II, were described only and have no cached abstract. Fitts & Seeger (1953) has a cached abstract, but it stops before the results, so it does not support the recorded finding that compatible mappings are faster and more accurate.

### Replication
The consistent-vs-varied-mapping effect (Schneider & Shiffrin 1977 / Shiffrin & Schneider 1977) is one of the most heavily replicated paradigms in cognitive psychology's automaticity literature (visual search, categorization, skill acquisition), by broad consensus of the field, though no single named large-scale pre-registered replication was located in this session (searched). Fitts & Seeger 1953's stimulus-response-compatibility effect is similarly a foundational, widely replicated paradigm in human factors. No failed large replication of either core paradigm was found.

### Backfire evidence
Sarter & Woods 1995 supplies the specific backfire mechanism the brief asked about: a fixed mapping applied in a context it fits poorly is not merely suboptimal but can actively mislead, because operators develop automatic, unreflective responses to a mark/control based on its usual meaning, and a context-dependent shift in what it means (a "mode") is exactly what evades detection by trained, attentive users — it is not a novice error. Applied to proof-actions: if the colour-language protocol were to let a colour's meaning shift between artifact types (e.g., "go deeper" on green in one context vs a different meaning on another page type), this predicts systematic misreadings precisely because the mapping usually holds, not despite it.

### Grade
**C** · transfer **analogical** · effect **n/a**
Rationale: The core automaticity evidence is unread. What remains is Norman's (1981) theory of action slips, which names mode errors as a category, and Sarter & Woods's (1995) review. The review draws on "empirical data from a series of studies of pilot-automation interaction" and finds that proliferating modes creates "new mode-related error forms". That supports the second half of the claim, that context-dependent meanings provoke mode errors, from a small set of cockpit studies. It does not support the first half, that constant mappings give faster and more accurate use. On the read evidence the grade is C. It would probably earn B once Schneider & Shiffrin (1977) is read, since that paradigm is widely replicated. Transfer is analogical: nothing tests hand marks on paper that an agent then carries out. Tie-break: none.

### Adversarial pass
confirmed: C stands. Only Norman (theory) and Sarter & Woods count, and the review's empirical cockpit studies support just the mode-error half, on few studies (C claim, no search run).
